The point of view

Words kept in natural, made and lit things

Most memory apps look like flashcards. This one should look like a place you return to: water at first light, a potter’s table, a window with the sun behind it.

Each board is one register of the app. Still Water is the daily practice: calm, open, quiet enough to think. Potter’s Clay is the family and the children: warm, handmade, patient. Living Stones is mastery: the six stone levels and the moment one is reached.

Every image is a public-domain work from the Art Institute of Chicago, not stock photography. Painting gives what your references were reaching for, a painted ground behind clean white cards, and a nineteenth-century landscape never looks like an ad. Each board opens with the verse its imagery comes from, so the look and the content come from the same place.

The one bold move: the background is alive. It drifts slowly, changes with the time of day, and lights up from behind when a level is reached. Everything in front of it stays still and plain.

Your references

Handhold (white cards on a painted sky)

What I see. A bold first word, then a muted rest of the sentence (“Guide. Handhold shows what to do…”), on frosted white cards over a painterly landscape.

What we take. Lesson instructions written the same way: “Recall. Say the whole verse from its reference.” The painting behind is the Still Water board.

QuizWiz (one question per card)

What I see. One large question per card, a 2×2 grid of answers, a different soft color for each card.

What we take. The meaning questions. The card color can come from the item’s current stone.

Meditation login (“How would you like to feel?”)

What I see. One question, stacked soft buttons, the chosen one warmed to peach, on a painted texture.

What we take. Onboarding and the planner: “What would you like to memorize?” with one choice warmed.

Family reflection card

What I see. Stacked faces, a response count and a date on a quiet card.

What we take. The family prompt (“At dinner tonight, ask together…”), with whose turn it is shown as faces.

Board I · Practice and review · 13 images

Still Water

“He leads me beside still waters. He restores my soul.” Psalm 23:2–3

The idea

This is where the daily work happens, so it should feel like sitting by water at first light. Every image here is mostly air or water: a narrow range of tones, soft edges, and motion that is only implied (mist lifting, a surface barely moving). That leaves calm space for a card of text to sit on, the way your Handhold reference floats white cards on a painted sky.

How the images were chosen

I kept works where at least half the frame is open sky, mist or water, where the tonal range is narrow enough for text to stay readable on top, and where something could move slowly without becoming a distraction. Anything with a strong focal subject was cut.

Where it shows up in the app

  • Lesson and review backgrounds, one painting per time of day: mist in the morning, gold at dusk, nocturne at bedtime.
  • Motion: two or three blurred layers drifting over 40–60 seconds, and a soft ripple when an answer lands. All of it off when the phone asks for reduced motion.
  • Feedback that blooms like watercolor instead of flashing red or green.

Board II · Family, children and the plan · 12 images

Potter’s Clay

“We are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand.” Isaiah 64:8

The idea

Memorizing as a family is handmade work: patient, a little imperfect, done together over months. This board is earth, ridges and glaze. It is the warm register of the app, for the family home, the children’s track and the planner, where the image of a long road into the mountains carries a long goal.

How the images were chosen

I looked for surfaces you want to touch (glaze, palette knife, ribbed clay), for mountains stacked in layers that suggest distance and a journey, and for objects that show the hand that made them. Nothing glossy or perfect.

Where it shows up in the app

  • Family home and the planner: layered ridges as the path toward a goal, one ridge per section.
  • The children’s track: flat shapes and few colors, in the manner of the woodblock prints.
  • Card and tile surfaces borrowed from glazes, with celadon as the main action color.

Board III · Mastery, levels and reward · 13 images

Living Stones

“You yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house.” 1 Peter 2:5

The idea

Light through color, and stones made precious by being set. This is the register for mastery: the six levels named for the foundation stones of Revelation 21, the moment a level is reached, and the treasury of verses kept for good. Stained glass carries the idea well: many small pieces, each held by lead, becoming one picture when light comes through.

How the images were chosen

I chose objects that either carry light through them (glass, crystal) or are polished stone set in gold, and whose colors map honestly onto the six levels. The museum pieces give real carnelian, real green jasper and real amethyst to match against. Medieval and Tiffany glass are here for their lead lines, which make a natural grid for interface.

Where it shows up in the app

  • The level badges: a round “roundel” per item, colored by its stone, set in a thin gold rim.
  • The level-up moment: glass lit slowly from behind, never confetti.
  • Progress as a strip of glass that fills pane by pane, and the family’s items gathered into one window.

Five products worth borrowing from

UX examples

Living backgrounds

Apple Weather

What it does. The backgrounds come in thousands of variations that follow the sun’s position, cloud and rain where you are, so the screen tells you something before you read a word.

What we take. Let the background follow the time of day: morning mist for new pieces, gold at dusk, a nocturne for the bedtime review. The scene itself says what kind of session this is.

Sources: 9to5Mac · MacRumors

Calm generative motion

Endel

What it does. Each soundscape has its own generative visual. The motion is gentle and slow, in a mostly monochrome interface.

What we take. Motion as atmosphere, not decoration. When a verse is read aloud, the water behind it can move with the voice, very slightly.

Sources: Endel · Screensdesign

Calm without penalties

Monument Valley

What it does. No timers, no enemies, no penalties for failure, in a restrained pastel world where nothing is there only to look pretty.

What we take. No timers or lives in lessons. A wrong answer teaches instead of punishing. Every ornament has a job.

Sources: Wallpaper · InVision

Layered landscapes and light

Alto’s Odyssey

What it does. A minimal art style made of layered landscapes, with dynamic lighting and weather that change the mood of each place.

What we take. Build backgrounds from flat layers (the woodblock approach) so they can shift with depth and light. Cheap to render, rich to look at.

Sources: Alto’s Odyssey press · MacStories

The closest existing app

Verses: Bible Memory

What it does. A progression of six memory games from first exposure to full recall, plus a “memory health” score that prompts a review when it drops.

What we take. The baseline to beat. What we add: levels that require passes on different days, a planner, a family, and a look that feels made for Scripture.

Sources: App Store · Learn of Christ review

What levels, gems and points can look like

Levels and points

The aim is feedback that tells the truth about memory, not confetti. These three show the pieces: gems as objects you earn, medals with weight and a record behind them, and a history that grows.

Gems as earned objects

Opal

What it does. Focus Gems are the heart of the app: rendered 3D gems unlocked at milestones, each with a name, and shareable. Opal says only about 2% of users reach the highest one.

What we take. The closest model for the stone levels: a gem you can turn and look at, rarity told honestly. One user complained that equipping a gem changed nothing visible, so in our app an item’s level should show everywhere that item appears.

Sources: Opal features · Milestone reveal · Review

Medals with weight

Apple Fitness awards

What it does. Awards are medals with distinct shapes and materials, and you can open one full screen and rotate it.

What we take. Each level gets its own cut and stone, not just a color. Tap a badge to turn it in the light and see the date and the three recalls that earned it.

Sources: MacRumors · Apple developer forum

A visible history

Forest

What it does. A tree grows while you focus, and each finished session joins a forest that is your record over time.

What we take. Keep what was earned visible as a growing thing: a window that fills with roundels, a wall of living stones. But skip its punishment (a tree that dies when you leave): for children, loss should never be the motivator. Critics also note people game the streak, which is why our levels need passes on separate days.

Sources: Forest · Good UX

For the next round

Decisions for you

  1. Lead with one board, or use all three as registers (practice, family, mastery) as proposed?
  2. Painting or photography behind the cards? This page argues for painting. Photography would feel more modern and less like a museum.
  3. How much motion: still images, slow drift, or backgrounds that follow the time of day?
  4. Does the app have a name yet? The direction could carry one (“Still Waters”, “Living Stones”).
