# Heir: a memory audit, and how to use sleep

Written from the point of view of a Christian cognitive neuroscientist of long-term memory: what Heir's
lessons do well, where they work against how memory consolidates, and what to add, above all an
optional, relaxed review before bed and a short recall on waking.

> "These words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your
> children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and **when you
> lie down, and when you rise**." Deuteronomy 6:6–7

Scripture already prescribes the schedule memory science recommends: rehearse before sleep, recall on
waking, repeat across days, and teach it aloud to someone else.

---

## 1. What Heir already does well

| In the app | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Recall from memory (typing, saying, first letters) | Retrieving strengthens memory far more than rereading (the testing effect; Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). |
| Spaced reviews scheduled by FSRS | Spacing across days beats cramming (Cepeda et al., 2006 and 2008). |
| Scaffolds fade: word bank, then blanks, then first letters, then the whole answer | Generation and "desirable difficulty" (Bjork): effortful recall that still succeeds builds the strongest memories. |
| Saying it aloud | The production effect: words said aloud are remembered better than words read silently (MacLeod, 2010). |
| Meaning questions | Understanding gives the words something to hang on; deeper processing lasts longer. |
| Interleaving, never two steps of a kind in a row | Mixing items and tasks forces discrimination and better long-term retention. |
| Lock-in of a unit from memory | Cumulative retrieval of the whole set, close to successive relearning (Rawson and Dunlosky). |
| Practice that picks the most at-risk items first | The right target: items on the edge of forgetting. |
| Sunday as rest, never breaking the run | Removes anxiety, which harms both sleep and learning. |

## 2. Where the current design works against memory

1. **The first interval is set by performance during learning.** When an item is first learned, the
   session's own score is fed to FSRS as if it were a review. Moments after studying, everything feels
   known (short-term memory), so the item usually gets "Good" and a first gap of about three days. A newly
   learned text should be recalled **the next day, after one night's sleep**: that first delayed recall is
   the strongest predictor and strengthener of long-term memory.
2. **Recognition and recall are graded the same.** A slip on "spot the wrong word" or "match" counts
   like a failure to recall the whole answer, while a clean recognition step counts as success. Only
   **recall** (type it, say it, first letters) should drive the schedule.
3. **Only three grades are used** (Again, Hard, Good). Fast, error-free recall of a mature item is never
   "Easy", so well-known items are reviewed more often than needed, which crowds the daily load.
4. **Fuzz is off.** Items learned together come due together, in clumps. Fuzz spreads them across days.
5. **Spacing is counted in lessons, not days.** "Practice every third lesson" assumes one lesson per
   sitting. Three lessons in one evening mean three new questions with no sleep between them. Spacing
   across sleep is what consolidates.
6. **Lock-in comes straight after the fifth question is learned,** when that question is still in
   short-term memory. Locking in the next day (after sleep) tests real retention, and the effort of
   recalling it is itself what strengthens it.
7. **Nothing knows the time of day.** Evening and morning are the two most valuable moments for
   memorization, and the app treats them like any other.
8. **The retry of a missed step happens at the end of the same session.** That is right for feedback, but
   should not count toward the schedule (it is massed, not spaced).

## 3. Sleep and memory, briefly

- During deep (slow-wave) sleep the hippocampus replays the day's new memories, and sleep spindles help
  transfer them into lasting storage in the neocortex (reviews: Diekelmann and Born, 2010; Rasch and Born,
  2013).
- Material studied **shortly before sleep** is retained better than the same material studied in the
  morning and followed by a day awake, with all its interference (for example, Gais, Lucas and Born, 2006,
  on vocabulary).
- **Learn in the evening, sleep, recall in the morning:** this pattern needed less relearning and kept
  more a week later than the same amount of practice within one day (Mazza et al., 2016).
- Sleep seems to favour memories that are **meaningful, recently retrieved, and not yet solid**
  (for example, Schapiro et al., 2018, on weaker memories). Those are exactly the items to review at
  bedtime.
- **Interference:** new, similar material learned after the review competes with it. The last thing
  rehearsed should be the thing you want to keep.
- **Cueing during sleep** with sounds linked to learning (targeted memory reactivation; for example,
  Rudoy et al., 2009) works in the lab. Playing audio through the night risks disturbing sleep, so it
  should not be built into the app.

## 4. The proposal: "When you lie down" and "When you rise"

### 4.1 "When you lie down": an optional evening review (3–5 minutes)

- **When:** offered from the evening onward, or an hour before a bedtime the person sets. Never required,
  never nagging. It keeps the day like any lesson, but missing it costs nothing.
- **What:** four to six items. First, the questions learned today, since those gain most from sleep.
  Then two or three items whose chance of recall is middling (about 60–85%), the ones on the edge. No new
  questions, and nothing confusable with tonight's items.
- **How it feels:**
  - A night theme: warm, dim and low contrast, with the window glowing quietly. No confetti, no timers,
    no scores.
  - **Listen and answer**, for eyes closed or the phone face down. The app reads the question, waits
    about 6–8 seconds while you answer aloud or under your breath, then reads the answer once.
    Whispering still gives most of the production effect.
  - Or **read and recall** on screen: the question, then tap to reveal phrase by phrase, gently.
  - A simple honest check: "Did you have it? Yes / Partly / Not yet." No typing at night.
- **How it ends:**
  - The last answer turned into a one-line prayer, then Psalm 4:8: "In peace I will both lie down and
    sleep; for you alone, O Lord, make me dwell in safety."
  - The window dims. No "next lesson" button: the session is over.
- **Scheduling:** it counts as a retrieval, but gently. A "Not yet" queues the item for the morning, not
  an "Again" that resets its schedule.

### 4.2 "When you rise": a morning recall (about 60–90 seconds)

- **What:** the next morning, recall last night's items once from the question alone, by saying or
  typing it.
- **This is the measurement that matters:** a delayed recall after sleep is the honest rating for FSRS.
  It replaces the inflated rating from the learning session.
- **The moment:** the panes lit last night brighten in the window, and a single morning verse: Psalm
  5:3, "O Lord, in the morning you hear my voice."

### 4.3 A daily rhythm

| When | What | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | Rise: recall last night's items | 1 min |
| Day | The main lesson: one new question, practice, due reviews | 5–8 min |
| Evening | Lie down: today's new question and a few on the edge | 3–5 min |

## 5. Changes to scheduling and grading

1. **First review after sleep.** When an item is first learned, don't rate it from the session. Set its
   first review for the next day, or turn on FSRS's short-term steps, and let the morning recall (or the
   next day's lesson) give the first real rating.
2. **Grade on recall only.** Rate from type, say and first-letters steps. Recognition steps (match, spot,
   word bank) give feedback but don't move the schedule.
3. **Use all four grades:** Easy for fast, error-free full recall of a mature item; Good for correct
   recall; Hard for recall with one slip or a hint; Again for failure. Time to answer is a useful signal.
4. **Turn fuzz on** in the live app, so reviews spread out.
5. **Space by days, not lessons:**
   - At most two new questions a day, or one for children.
   - Extra lessons on the same day become practice, and can be offered as "Keep going? Practice only".
6. **Lock in after sleep.** When a unit's fifth question is learned, schedule the lock-in for the next day:
   "Tomorrow: lock in Questions 1–5."
7. **Don't count same-session retries** toward the schedule.
8. **Target retention:** 0.9 is right for now. Consider 0.92–0.95 for questions the person marks as most
   important, or for children preparing to recite.
9. **Maintenance mode:** once a whole Part is mastered, a daily two-minute "keep the window lit" review
   samples a few items across everything learned, so nothing fades over the years.

## 6. Changes to lessons

1. **Recall from the question, as it will be used.** Catechizing is call and response: someone asks the
   question and you answer. Practise more "question shown, answer from memory", including audio of the
   question read aloud.
2. **Contrast confusable answers.** Questions with similar wording interfere with each other. Add
   occasional "which question says this?" steps between near neighbours, building on the believable
   mix-ups already in `swaps.ts`.
3. **Fade scaffolds by performance, not by count.** Move a person to full recall as soon as they succeed;
   hold them back only if they struggle.
4. **Explain it in your own words** (spoken, ungraded). Self-explanation deepens understanding and
   memory, and is how a child shows they mean it.
5. **Proof texts:** link each answer to one key verse, and occasionally ask "which verse supports this?"
   This connects the catechism to Scripture and gives a second retrieval route.
6. **Pray the answer:** an optional closing line turning the answer into prayer. Personal meaning and
   emotion strengthen consolidation, and it is the point of memorizing.
7. **The window as a memory palace.** Each question owns real panes in a real scene, so tie them: "Q6
   lives in the halo." Placing words in a picture (dual coding, the method of loci) gives each answer a
   visual address.
8. **Melody, optional:** simple sung settings help word-perfect recall, especially for children
   (Wallace, 1994). They could come later as audio.
9. **Keep sessions short:** 5–8 minutes is right. Attention and accuracy fall after that, and the planner
   already switches to practice when accuracy dips.

## 7. Family and children

- **Children's sleep is rich in deep sleep,** so a bedtime catechism is ideal for them: a parent asks, the
  child answers, then lights out. A **"Family night"** mode reads the question aloud for the parent to
  ask, records nothing, and lights the child's panes.
- **Teaching others** (the protégé effect): when a parent leads, both remember better.
- **Morning recall at the breakfast table,** in the "When you rise" spirit.

## 8. Reminders, gently

- One evening reminder at a time the person chooses, for example 30–60 minutes before bed. Optional,
  with no guilt language.
- An optional morning reminder for the one-minute recall.
- Never a reminder late at night: the night session should never cost sleep.

## 9. What to measure

- **Next-day recall rate** of items learned the day before: the best early signal.
- **7-day and 30-day recall** from FSRS reviews.
- **The bedtime effect:** recall the next morning for items reviewed at night versus items not reviewed
  at night, within the same person, so they serve as their own comparison.
- **Load:** reviews per day as the catechism grows. It should stay under about 5 minutes.

## 10. Order to build

1. **Scheduling fixes,** small and high impact: first review after sleep, grade on recall only, four
   grades, fuzz on, new questions capped per day, lock-in after sleep.
2. **"When you lie down":** the evening review in a night theme, read-and-recall first, then the audio
   mode.
3. **"When you rise":** the morning recall feeding FSRS.
4. **Question-first recall and contrast steps** in lessons.
5. **Proof texts and "pray the answer".**
6. **Family night mode.**
7. **Maintenance mode** once Parts are mastered.
