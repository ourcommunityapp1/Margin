## 1. What core method do the standard seminary textbooks teach?

### Takeaway
All six textbooks share one backbone. The reading is grammatical-historical and focused on the author. It first establishes what the text meant to its first readers, through its literary context, historical setting and genre, and only then moves to what it means now. The whole canon is the final context ("Scripture interprets Scripture"), and application comes from principles the text itself carries. The books differ mainly in their metaphor and emphasis:
- **Fee & Stuart** frame it as "then" and "now" and organize by genre.
- **Duvall & Hays** use a five-step "Interpretive Journey" that crosses a "principlizing bridge."
- **Osborne** describes an iterative "spiral" from text to context.
- **Gorman** sets out seven "elements" that end in theological reflection.
- **Kaiser & Silva** defend authorial meaning against reader-centred theory.
- **Klein, Blomberg & Hubbard** give the fullest reference treatment.

That is the Logos/Faithlife wording, and Goodreads matches it; a LibraryThing review quotes the shorter "A text cannot mean what it never meant." The book treats what the text originally meant to its first audience as the starting point for what it means to readers today (Logos listing, pp. 34–35). — [Logos](https://www.logos.com/product/50318); [Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/420678); [LibraryThing](https://www.librarything.com/a/9335)

  - OT narratives; Acts (how far historical narrative sets precedent); the Gospels; the parables;
  - the Law; the Prophets; the Psalms; "Wisdom: Then and Now"; and "Revelation: Images of Judgment and Hope."

**J. Scott Duvall & J. Daniel Hays, *Grasping God's Word* (Zondervan; 3rd ed. 2012; 4th ed. Zondervan Academic, ISBN 978-0-310-10918-1)**
- The Interpretive Journey has five steps:
  1. **Grasp the text in their town**: what it meant to the biblical audience.
  2. **Measure the width of the river to cross**: the differences between them and us.
  3. **Cross the principlizing bridge**: state the theological principle.
  4. **Consult the biblical map**: test the principle against the rest of Scripture.
  5. **Grasp the text in our town**: how individual Christians today should live out the principle.

  Some summaries of the 3rd edition count four steps plus a final application step (diagram cited at p. 196). The 4th edition is marketed as "a five-step Interpretive Journey." — [Shepherd's Path, 2024](https://shepherdspath.substack.com/p/the-path-31124); [The Devotional Guy, 2025](https://thedevotionalguy.blog/2025/10/13/the-interpretative-journey-a-bible-study-method/); [Training Leaders International review](https://trainingleadersinternational.org/jgc/111/grasping-gods-word-a-hands-on-approach-to-reading-interpreting-and-applying-the-bible); [Ethnos360 Bible Institute](https://e360bible.org/how-to-study-the-bible/the-interpretive-journey/); [4th-ed. listing](https://www.shakes.cz/ebook/978-0-310-10918-1)
- Student guides to the book list five tests for a valid theological principle:
  1. It is reflected in the text.
  2. It is timeless, not tied to a specific situation.
  3. It is not culturally bound.
  4. It corresponds to the teaching of the rest of Scripture.
  5. It is relevant to both the biblical and the contemporary audience.

  — [CliffsNotes study note on GGW assignment 2-2](https://www.cliffsnotes.com/study-notes/1721332); [Ethnos360](https://e360bible.org/how-to-study-the-bible/the-interpretive-journey/); [church handout "Grasping God's Word: The Basics of the Journey"](https://cdn.subsplash.com/documents/T8N4NK/_source/19f3cf7d-d1d7-4a6e-9d49-a0e960fff8bd/document.pdf)

- Critique: Stephen Barkley says the book presents the principlizing bridge as if it were the only method, and assumes interpretation is done individually rather than in community. — [Barkley, 2021](https://stephenbarkley.com/2021/08/25/grasping-gods-word-scott-j-duvall-and-j-daniel-hayes/)

**Grant R. Osborne, *The Hermeneutical Spiral* (IVP Academic; 1991; revised & expanded 2006)**
- **The spiral.** Hermeneutics is "a spiral from text to context." The reader moves between the horizon of the text and the horizon of the reader, and each pass comes closer to the intended meaning and its significance today.
- **Edition notes.** The 2006 edition added chapters on Old Testament law and on the New Testament's use of the Old. The book won a 1993 *Christianity Today* Critics' Choice award.

  — [IVP Academic](https://ivpress.com/the-hermeneutical-spiral)
- **Themelios review.** It summarizes the book as follows:
  - Hermeneutics combines exegesis ("what the text meant") with contextualization ("how it applies").
  - The reader "repeatedly tests one's pre-understandings by detailed interaction with the text, which then modifies one's interpretation," and checks the result against the wider Christian and scholarly community.
  - There are three parts: general hermeneutics, genre, and applied hermeneutics.
  - Appendices ask whether textual meaning can be equated with authorial intent, which is where the book engages postmodern challenges.

  — [Themelios review](https://thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/the-hermeneutical-spiral-a-comprehensive-introduction-to-biblical-interpretation)
- **Two kinds of context.** Historical context covers authorship, date and background. Logical context widens from the immediate passage to the whole Bible and the passage's genre. — [Lee Jared Garcia review, 2019](https://leejaredgarcia.com/2019/08/13/the-hermeneutical-spiral-by-grant-osborne-a-book-review/)

- The publisher calls it a textbook of guidelines for interpreting Scripture. **Walter C. Kaiser Jr. & Moisés Silva, *An Introduction to Biblical Hermeneutics: The Search for Meaning* (Zondervan; rev. & expanded 2nd ed.)**
- **Peter K. Nelson's Themelios review** makes three points:
  - The co-authors deliberately hold differing views on many issues but agree on the authority of Scripture and "the primacy of authorial meaning."
  - They respond to the shift in contemporary hermeneutics "away from the notions of objective truth and authorial meaning, and toward reader-centred and relevance-based approaches."
  - They hold that the task is incomplete until the believer's life has been "touched and changed by the principles expressed in the biblical text."

Raju Kunjummen ("The Single Intent of Scripture—Critical Examination of a Theological Construct," *Grace Theological Journal* 7.1, 1986) argues that God's intent as author can sometimes go beyond the human author's intent. **Michael J. Gorman, *Elements of Biblical Exegesis: A Basic Guide for Students and Ministers***
- Exegesis has seven elements. Each gets its own chapter, with writing guidance and exercises:
  1. Survey.
  2. Contextual analysis.
  3. Formal analysis (form, structure, movement).
  4. Detailed analysis.
  5. Synthesis.
  6. Reflection (theological interpretation).
  7. Expansion and refinement (returning to the work after consulting other scholars).

  — [Themelios review](https://thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/elements-of-biblical-exegesis-a-basic-guide-for-students-and-ministers); [NNU library page for course BIBL 8090](https://library.nnu.edu/docriley/bibl8090)
- A Dallas Theological Seminary reviewer calls the method "somewhat eclectic," broadly "historical, literary, and theological." The reviewer praises its emphasis on application, which is "often left out of more formal treatments." — [DTS Voice review](https://voice.dts.edu/review/michael-j-gorman-elements-of-biblical-exegesis/)

**Evangelical consensus statements that encode the same method**
- *Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy* (ICBI, 1978), Art. XVIII, verbatim: "WE AFFIRM that the text of Scripture is to be interpreted by grammatico-historical exegesis, taking account of its literary forms and devices, and that Scripture is to interpret Scripture." — [Creeds.json text (source: DTS archive ICBI_1.pdf)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/chicago_statement_on_biblical_inerrancy.json)
- *Chicago Statement on Biblical Hermeneutics* (ICBI). It came out of a meeting in Chicago on 10–13 November 1982 and elaborates the 1978 statement.
  - **Art. XV** affirms reading the Bible by its "literal, or normal, sense," meaning the grammatico-historical sense the writer expressed. That reading takes account of figures of speech and literary forms. It denies any approach that gives Scripture a meaning the literal sense does not support.
  - **Art. XVIII** affirms that the Bible's interpretation of itself is always correct, "never deviating from, but rather elucidating, the single meaning of the inspired text."

### Inferences
- **The shared pipeline** across all six books runs in this order:
  1. Read the passage closely and observe its structure.
  2. Place it in its literary context, then its historical and cultural setting.
  3. Identify the genre and its conventions.
  4. Establish the meaning the author intended to convey to the original audience.
  5. Set it in its canonical and biblical-theological context ("Scripture interprets Scripture").
  6. Synthesize it theologically.
  7. Derive a principle and apply it.

  This is the popular "observation → interpretation → application" triad, made more detailed.
- **Meaning and application are kept apart.** Osborne's exegesis/contextualization, Duvall & Hays's "their town" and "our town," and Kaiser & Silva's insistence that application completes the task all separate *meaning*, which is fixed and set by the author, from *significance or application*, which varies with the reader. An AI should keep these two labelled separately in what it says.
- **Iterative, not linear.** Osborne's spiral and Gorman's "expansion and refinement" both treat interpretation as revisable. For an AI, that means stating a reading, then checking it against the commentaries and the wider canon, and revising when they disagree, rather than producing one confident pass.
- **Theology belongs inside exegesis.** Gorman's explicit "reflection (theological interpretation)" step and Kaiser & Silva's added biblical-theology chapters show the textbooks now treat theology as an internal stage, which bridges to the theological interpretation movement in §5.

## 2. Which exegetical fallacies are most cited (Carson, Silva, Barr; proof-texting)?

### Takeaway
The modern evangelical account of word-study errors goes back to James Barr's 1961 critique of "biblical theology" word studies, especially Kittel's dictionary. D. A. Carson turned it into a student checklist of about fifty fallacies in four families (word, grammar, logic, presupposition/history). Moisés Silva then grounded it in lexical semantics. The core rules:
- A word means what it means **in its context**. Etymology, a word's whole range of senses, later senses and theological "concepts" must not be poured into a single occurrence.
- Do not lift a verse out of its context to prove a point it does not make.

### Cited Findings
- **Barr: "illegitimate totality transfer."** James Barr, *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961). The error is that "the various meanings a word has in all its contexts in the Bible are all read into a single passage" (Barr p. 218, as summarized by Vern Poythress). — [By Faith We Understand (Poythress summary)](https://byfaithweunderstand.com/2008/11/07/poythress-summarizes-james-barr/page/2/?et_blog); [Silva review confirming Barr's 1961 date](https://www.wisluthsem.org/biblical-words-and-their-meaning/)
- **Barr on Kittel.** Barr charged Kittel's *Theological Dictionary of the New Testament* with constantly confusing words with concepts. Its editors billed it as a word dictionary, yet its articles sketched far-reaching concepts by discussing what words refer to. — [UZH NT blog](https://www.uzh.ch/blog/theologie-nt/?p=667); [Scriptura wiki](https://nyc.scriptura.org/w/The_Semantics_of_Biblical_Language)
- **Two examples Barr's critique used.**
  - *ἀγάπη:* the love shown by God was read into every occurrence of the word.
  - *dabar:* it sometimes means "word" and sometimes "matter/thing." Barr faulted T. F. Torrance for making it mean both at once.

  Barr accepts that a total biblical *concept* can be assembled from all the uses of a term. His objection is to treating that total as the word's meaning in each context. — [First Things, "Totality transfer"](https://firstthings.com/totality-transfer); [Poythress summary](https://byfaithweunderstand.com/2008/11/07/poythress-summarizes-james-barr/page/2/?et_blog); [Puritanboard thread](https://www.puritanboard.com/threads/semantics-of-biblical-language-james-barr.95298/latest)
- **Carson, *Exegetical Fallacies* (Baker).** About 50 fallacies in four chapters: word-study, grammatical, logical, and presuppositional/historical. — [9Marks review](https://www.9marks.org/review/book-review-exegetical-fallacies-by-d-a-carson/); [Wisconsin Lutheran Seminary review](https://www.wisluthsem.org/review-exegetical-fallacies/); [Josef Urban review (RBS)](https://rbs.org/news/d-a-carsons-exegetical-fallacies-a-review-by-josef-urban.html)
- **Word-study fallacies:**
  - *Root fallacy:* it "presupposes that every word actually has a meaning bound up with its shape or components." Carson's example is ἀπόστολος: NT usage means a special representative or messenger, not etymologically "someone sent out" (p. 30).
  - *Semantic anachronism:* it "occurs when a late use of a word is read back into earlier literature" (p. 33).
  - *Appeal to unknown or unlikely meanings:* p. 37.

  — [9Marks review](https://www.9marks.org/review/book-review-exegetical-fallacies-by-d-a-carson/); [Credo House, "Common errors in Bible interpretation"](https://credohouse.org/blog/common-errors-in-bible-interpretation); [WLS review](https://www.wisluthsem.org/review-exegetical-fallacies/)
- **Totality transfer as "overload."** George Guthrie calls illegitimate totality transfer the "overload fallacy," loading several possible meanings into one use. His example is χάρις in Eph 2:8. — [Guthrie, "Doin' the Wonky with Words," 2016](https://www.georgehguthrie.com/new-blog/2016/7/24/doin-the-wonky-with-words-4-word-study-missteps-part-2)
- **Grammatical, logical and presuppositional fallacies:**
  - *Grammatical:* tense, mood, voice, conditionals and the article. The aorist "rarely lives up to the hype" often attached to it (pp. 68–75).
  - *Logical:* a list of eighteen common argumentative fallacies, including world-view confusion (p. 103), appeals to emotion (p. 106) and appeals to authority (p. 122).
  - *Presuppositional/historical:* includes imposing socio-cultural categories and theories on the text (pp. 125–128).

  — [WLS review](https://www.wisluthsem.org/review-exegetical-fallacies/); [RBS review](https://rbs.org/news/d-a-carsons-exegetical-fallacies-a-review-by-josef-urban.html)
- **Silva, *Biblical Words and Their Meaning: An Introduction to Lexical Semantics*** (1983; revised 1994).
  - It opens with a critique of the 20th-century tendency to read whole theologies into the internal structure of a single word ("etymological theology").
  - It builds on Barr's critiques of illegitimate totality transfer, and on his view that NT theology is carried by word combinations and sentences more than by isolated words.
  - It questions the TDNT's mixing of a word's history with its meaning.
  - It notes that compounds and derivatives can lose their motivation (p. 49).
  - The revised edition adds a bibliographical essay and an appendix by Karen Jobes on Greek worship verbs.

  — [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/10407106); [WLS review (Isaac Cherney, 2019)](https://www.wisluthsem.org/biblical-words-and-their-meaning/); [Logos 2nd-ed. page](https://www.logos.com/product/41551/biblical-words-and-their-meaning-2nd-ed)
- **Proof-texting: definition.** It means lifting a verse out of its context to support an idea the passage itself does not teach, or citing proof texts that do not actually support the statement they are attached to. — [Servants of Grace](https://servantsofgrace.org/reading-scripture-rightly-proof-texting/); [Billious Oz Substack](https://billiousoz.substack.com/p/a-note-about-proof-texts)
- **Proof-texting: proper order of cross-references.** Cross-referencing should be "employed as a check upon interpretation." The problem comes when Text B is brought in before Text A has been exegeted. — [SharperIron](https://www.sharperiron.org/node/38737)
- The Aquila Report warns that appeals to "parallel passages" can let eisegesis creep in. It recommends checking the same book first, then the same author, then other biblical writers. — [Aquila Report](https://theaquilareport.com/scripture-interprets-scripture/)
- **Proof-texting: contested edge.** Defenders of confessional theology distinguish proof-texting from the Westminster method. That method reads a passage in context first and then draws inferences by "good and necessary consequence." An Adventist academic paper summarizes Allen and Swain's three main objections to proof-texting: it does not honour the specific context, it assigns unwarranted meaning to biblical language, and it favours readings that serve doctrinal interests. — [Cornerstone RBC](https://cornerstonerbc.com/blog/are-we-using-the-right-approach-to-the-new-testament); [AIIAS paper](https://www.aiias.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/sample-academicpaper.pdf); [JAAS article](https://journals.aiias.edu/jaas/article/download/695/627)

### Inferences
- **Relevance to Margin's sources.** Henry, Barnes, Clarke and Spurgeon all wrote before Barr's 1961 critique. Their comments may contain etymological "root" arguments, or sermon-style word studies that load a word's whole range of senses into one verse. The AI should never repeat a commentator's etymology or "the Greek literally means…" claim as settled fact. It can report it as "Clarke notes…" and, where it matters, add that modern lexical method defines words by their use in context.
- **The same failure modes are typical of LLMs.** Sermon-style word studies, a common source of these fallacies, are plentiful online. Rules worth stating in the prompt:
  - Define a word by how it is used *in this passage*.
  - Name one sense, not a stack of senses.
  - Never use later English derivatives to explain a Greek or Hebrew word (semantic anachronism).
  - Distinguish the *word* from the theological *concept*.
- **Cross-references come last.** They support the passage's own sense rather than replacing it, and should be given in the Aquila Report's order (same book, same author, then the rest of the canon). When the AI draws a doctrinal inference across texts, it should show the steps of the inference rather than list verse references.

## 3. What genre-specific guidance is standard?

### Takeaway
Standard evangelical guidance is to read each genre by its own conventions:
- **Narrative** mostly *describes* and illustrates. It does not prescribe, and God is its hero.
- **OT law** is covenant stipulation. How it binds Christians depends on covenant and fulfilment in Christ; this is a contested area.
- **Psalms** are prayers and poetry that become God's word *to* us.
- **Wisdom** is observational.
- **Prophets** enforce the covenant, mostly speaking to their own generation.
- **Apocalyptic** speaks in images of judgment and hope that its first readers understood.
- **Epistles** are occasional letters whose original situation must be reconstructed before their teaching is universalized.

Getting the genre wrong is one of the main ways readers misapply Scripture.

### Cited Findings
- **Epistles.** They are occasional letters addressing specific first-century situations, so the reader must grasp the original audience's circumstances. Fee & Stuart model the process on 1 Corinthians. — [Bookey summary](https://www.bookey.app/book/how-to-read-the-bible-for-all-its-worth)
- **OT narrative: Fee & Stuart's principles** (as reproduced in course notes):
  - An OT narrative usually does not directly teach a doctrine. It usually illustrates doctrine taught propositionally elsewhere.
  - Narratives record what happened, not necessarily what should have happened.
  - Characters are often imperfect, and their actions are not automatically models.
  - Narratives "are not allegories or stories filled with hidden meanings."
  - "In the final analysis, God is the hero of all biblical narratives."

  Secondary sources disagree on page numbers (p. 106 vs pp. 83–84). — [CIU lesson notes on OT narrative](https://ciu.edu/sites/default/files/20212021/JunJun/Page/Lesson%208%20Notes-Interpreting%20Old%20Testament%20Narrative%20and%20Bible%20Translations.pdf); [Kevin Halloran](https://www.kevinhalloran.net/how-to-interpret-biblical-narrative/amp/); [FreeBibleCommentary](https://freebiblecommentary.org/HTML_Common/old_testament_narrative.html); [Bookey PDF summary](https://cdn.bookey.app/files/pdf/book/en/how-to-read-the-bible-for-all-its-worth.pdf); [GraceQuotes](https://gracequotes.org/quote/old-testament-narratives-are-not-just-stories-about-people-who-lived-in-old-testament-times-they-are-first-and-foremost-stories-about-what-god-did-to-and-through-those-people-in-contrast-to-human-na/)
- **Three levels of narrative.** Each individual OT narrative (bottom level) is part of Israel's history (middle level), which is part of the ultimate narrative of God's creation and redemption (top level). — [CIU lesson notes](https://ciu.edu/sites/default/files/20212021/JunJun/Page/Lesson%208%20Notes-Interpreting%20Old%20Testament%20Narrative%20and%20Bible%20Translations.pdf); [Kevin Halloran](https://www.kevinhalloran.net/how-to-interpret-biblical-narrative/amp/)

  - *Hermeneutics*, Art. XV: literal-sense interpretation "will take account of all figures of speech and literary forms."

### Inferences
Below is a genre table an AI could follow. It is synthesized from the findings above; rows marked † rest partly on unverified Fee & Stuart details listed under Gaps.

| Genre | Default reading posture | Error to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| OT narrative | Ask first "what is God doing?"; place the story on the three narrative levels; draw lessons through doctrine taught plainly elsewhere | Treating every action as a model ("be like Samson"); allegorizing details; reading description as command |
| Acts / historical narrative | Separate what is described from what is commanded; look for patterns Luke clearly intends | Turning one-off events into norms for all churches† |
| Law | Treat as covenant stipulations given to Israel; ask how Christ and the new covenant fulfil, continue or end each law. Reformed users will expect WCF 19's moral/ceremonial/judicial grid and "general equity" | Applying Sinai stipulations directly, or dismissing the moral law wholesale (the second contradicts WCF 19.5–6) |
| Psalms | Treat as prayer and poetry; let lament, praise and imprecation stand as prayer; parallelism and imagery are not doctrinal propositions | Pulling doctrine from poetic imagery word by word; skipping lament |
| Proverbs / wisdom | Treat as general observations about how life usually goes under God | Turning a proverb into an unconditional promise† |
| Prophets | Identify the covenant charge to the original audience first; then any fulfilment in Christ and the church as the NT shows it | "Newspaper exegesis"; assuming most prophecy is still in the future† |
| Apocalyptic (Revelation, parts of Daniel) | Treat as images of judgment and hope that the first readers could understand | Decoding symbols into current events; presenting one millennial or tribulation scheme as the only Christian view |
| Gospels / parables | Read each Gospel's account in its own literary context and in light of the kingdom† | Harmonizing too early; allegorizing every detail of a parable† |
| Epistles | Reconstruct the occasion; separate culture-bound particulars from transferable principles (Duvall & Hays tests) | Universalizing occasional instructions without asking what situation they addressed |

- **Apparent discrepancies.** ICBI Art. XIII gives an evangelically acceptable way to handle round numbers, topical order, free quotation and observational language. The AI can explain these as literary conventions rather than "errors," while admitting where interpreters disagree.

## 4. How do the leading works on expository preaching and teaching move from text to application?

### Takeaway
The leading homiletics texts agree on four points:
- Application must come from, and stay controlled by, the passage's *one main idea* (Robinson's "big idea").
- It must bridge the biblical and modern worlds, being authoritative on biblical principles but tentative on specific contemporary applications (Stott).
- It should name the human condition the text addresses and the grace the text supplies, rather than moralizing (Chapell's "Fallen Condition Focus").
- It should place the passage in redemptive history culminating in Christ, but only after establishing its original meaning (Greidanus and the redemptive-historical school).

Older Reformed standards already require the same things: reading with "attention to the matter and scope" and preaching "plainly… faithfully… wisely, applying… to the necessities and capacities of the hearers" (Westminster Larger Catechism).

### Cited Findings
- **Haddon Robinson, *Biblical Preaching* (Baker; 2nd ed. 2001 cited, pp. 21, 33–35).** Expository preaching is "the communication of a biblical concept, derived from and transmitted through a historical, grammatical, and literary study of a passage in its context, which the Holy Spirit first applies to the personality and experience of the preacher, then through the preacher, applies to the hearers." Wording varies slightly between quoting sources. — [Evangelical Focus](https://evangelicalfocus.com/biblical-preaching/15388/haddon-robinson-definition-of-expository-preaching); [SharperIron, "Haddon Robinson and 'Big Idea' Preaching"](https://sharperiron.org/node/35527)
- **Robinson's big idea.** A sermon explains, interprets or applies "a single dominant idea supported by other ideas, all drawn from one passage or several passages of Scripture." "A sermon should be a bullet, not a buckshot." Robinson criticizes sermons that comment on words and phrases with every sub-theme and word given equal weight. — [SharperIron](https://sharperiron.org/node/35527); [PreachingSource, "The One Homiletics Book Every Preacher Should Own: Part 4"](https://preachingsource.com/blog/the-one-homiletic-book-every-preacher-should-own-part-4/)
- Robinson's approach also generated a festschrift-style volume, *The Big Idea of Biblical Preaching: Connecting the Bible to People*. — [Baker Publishing Group](https://www.bakerpublishinggroup.com/books/the-big-idea-of-biblical-preaching/276352)
- **John Stott, *Between Two Worlds* (Eerdmans).**
  - Preaching is bridge-building from the biblical world to the modern mind, and the sermon must be anchored at both ends.
  - Preaching has two aspects: exposition of the Bible, and communication, meaning application of biblical truth to today's world.
  - A reviewer quotes Stott's conclusion that bridge-building preaching "will be authoritative in expounding biblical principles, but tentative in applying them to the complex issues of the day." This is a quotation via a reviewer; verify it against the book.

  — [Graceworks review](https://graceworks.com.sg/between-two-worlds/); [LibraryThing review](https://www.librarything.com/work/1528833); [Albert Mohler interview with Stott](https://albertmohler.com/?p=21770)
- A 2017 review of the Eerdmans republication says preachers now live "between three worlds," with the postmodern world as a third horizon. — [Ministry Magazine](https://www.ministrymagazine.org/archive/2020/01/Resources)
- **Bryan Chapell, *Christ-Centered Preaching: Redeeming the Expository Sermon*.** The Fallen Condition Focus is "the mutual human condition that contemporary believers share with those to or about whom the text was written that requires the grace of the passage for God's people to glorify and enjoy him." Reviews cite p. 50 and the Logos 3rd-edition listing p. 30; one review reads "to or for."
  - Chapell holds that every passage contains an FCF, explicitly or implicitly, and that it links today's hearers to the original audience.
  - The FCF is raised in the sermon's introduction, and the body then shows how God addresses it.

  — [SharperIron review](https://sharperiron.org/node/14383); [Ordained Servant review (OPC)](https://opc.org/OS/html/V7/2h.html); [Logos 3rd ed.](https://www.logos.com/product/175196/christ-centered-preaching-redeeming-the-expository-sermon-3rd-ed); [PreachingSource Part 3](https://preachingsource.com/blog/the-one-homiletics-book-every-preacher-should-own-part-3/)
- **Critiques of the FCF.**
  - A WELS reviewer says Chapell most often frames the FCF as "the solution to a perceived lack, a condition that can be righted by application of grace, and not necessarily as sin."
  - Zack Eswine sees the FCF as "equipped primarily as a tool for churched contexts."

  — [WLS review](https://www.wls.edu/review-christ-centered-preaching/); [Wikipedia, Bryan Chapell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Chapell)
- **Sidney Greidanus, *Preaching Christ from the Old Testament: A Contemporary Hermeneutical Method* (Eerdmans).** He gives seven legitimate ways from an OT text to Christ:
  1. Redemptive-historical progression.
  2. Promise-fulfilment.
  3. Typology.
  4. Analogy.
  5. Longitudinal themes.
  6. New Testament references.
  7. Contrast.

  He calls the approach a "redemptive-historical Christocentric method." He insists an OT passage must be understood in its original setting before it is read from a Christocentric perspective, and he is cautious about typology. — [Themelios review (Ecclesiastes volume)](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/preaching-christ-from-ecclesiastes-foundations-for-expository-sermons/); [Trevin Wax, TGC, "7 Ways of Preaching Christ from the Old Testament"](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/blogs/trevin-wax/7-ways-of-preaching-christ-from-the-old-testament/); [Ordained Servant review](https://opc.org/OS/html/V9/2f.html); [Greidanus, "Reflections on Preaching Christ from the OT," *SBJT* 22.3](https://www.galaxie.com/article/sbjt22-3-08)
- **Trevin Wax's summary.** Redemptive-historical progression is the broadest road, tracing redemption from Genesis to Revelation; for example, Noah's flood is read within Genesis's plotline about the promised seed. Promise-fulfilment is the most direct road. — [Trevin Wax, TGC](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/blogs/trevin-wax/7-ways-of-preaching-christ-from-the-old-testament/)
- **Westminster Larger Catechism (1647), verbatim:**
  - Q157, reading: the Scriptures are to be read "with diligence, and attention to the matter and scope of them; with meditation, application, self-denial, and prayer."
  - Q159, preaching: preachers "are to preach sound doctrine… plainly, not in the enticing words of man's wisdom… faithfully, making known the whole counsel of God; wisely, applying themselves to the necessities and capacities of the hearers."
  - Q160, hearers: they are to "examine what they hear by the scriptures… meditate, and confer of it; hide it in their hearts, and bring forth the fruit of it in their lives."
  - Q155: the Spirit makes "the reading, but especially the preaching of the word" effectual for "enlightening, convincing, and humbling sinners… building them up in grace."

  — [WLC, Creeds.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/westminster_larger_catechism.json)
- **Matthew Henry's two questions.** His working model of exposition asks two questions in order: "What is this?" and then "What is this to us?" — [Christian Study Library, "Matthew Henry on the Aim of Exposition"](https://www.christianstudylibrary.org/article/matthew-henry-aim-exposition)

### Inferences
- **A text-to-application sequence for Margin's audio sessions.** Each step maps to a source above.
  1. Read the passage aloud; the text comes first.
  2. Situate it: book, author, audience, occasion, place in the storyline.
  3. State the passage's one big idea in a single sentence (Robinson).
  4. Explain it, using a commentator to illuminate ("What is this?", Henry).
  5. Name the shared human need the text addresses and the grace it supplies (Chapell).
  6. Show its place in redemptive history and its link to Christ by one of Greidanus's legitimate routes, labelled as such.
  7. State the transferable principle (Duvall & Hays tests).
  8. Offer applications: confident on the principle, tentative on specific modern applications (Stott). Phrase them as questions for meditation and self-examination (WLC 157/160).
  9. Invite the listener to check what was said against the text (WLC 160).
- **Format fit.** "Bullet, not buckshot" suits a commute: one big idea per episode, stated early and restated at the end, fits listening better than verse-by-verse commentary.
- **Two real tensions that need a stated policy:**
  - *Christ-centred vs. exemplary application.* Greidanus and Chapell push for Christ- and grace-centred application and warn against moralism. Fee & Stuart's warning against reading narrative characters as models points the same way.
  - *Moral exhortation.* The WLS critique of the FCF, and the Reformed confessions' "moral law… rule of life" (WCF 19.6), keep plain moral exhortation legitimate.

  The AI should do both: name sin and duty plainly and ground obedience in grace. It should not force a Christ link where the canonical connection is not there.

- **Stott quotation.** Its exact wording and page are unverified; the UK edition is titled *I Believe in Preaching*.
- **Edition years (general knowledge, unverified):** Robinson 3rd ed. (2014); Chapell 3rd ed. (2018); Greidanus (1999); Stott (1982).

  - The Westminster *Directory for the Publique Worship of God* (1645), "Of the Preaching of the Word," with its Puritan "doctrine–reason–use" pattern and its insistence on bringing doctrine "home to special use, by application."
  - William Perkins, *The Art of Prophesying*.

  - The Dutch redemptive-historical vs. exemplary preaching controversy (subject of Greidanus's dissertation *Sola Scriptura*).
  - Critiques of "Christ in every text" preaching.

## 5. How do Reformation confessions frame interpretation, and what does the theological interpretation of Scripture (TIS) movement say about tradition, creeds and the rule of faith?

### Takeaway
**The confessions.** The Reformation confessions frame interpretation through six principles:
- Scripture is its own infallible interpreter: the clearer passages explain the less clear, and each text has a single sense ("not manifold, but one").
- What is necessary for salvation is clear enough for the unlearned "in a due use of the ordinary means."
- The Spirit's inward illumination is necessary for saving understanding.
- The original languages are the final court of appeal.
- Fathers, councils and every other human opinion are respected but tested by Scripture, the "Supreme Judge."
- The Swiss and Scots confessions add a "rule of faith and love/charity": no interpretation may contradict a principal article of faith or the law of love.

**TIS.** The TIS movement (Vanhoozer, Treier, Billings) argues for recovering reading *within* the church's rule of faith, the creeds and the ecclesial context. Evangelical critics such as D. A. Carson answer "Yes, but…": the movement is valuable but mixed, and risks over-privileging pre-critical exegesis.

### Cited Findings
**Westminster Confession of Faith (1647), ch. 1, verbatim**
- **1.6:** "The whole counsel of God… is either expressly set down in Scripture, or by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from Scripture: unto which nothing at any time is to be added, whether by new revelations of the Spirit, or traditions of men. Nevertheless we acknowledge the inward illumination of the Spirit of God to be necessary for the saving understanding of such things as are revealed in the Word."
- **1.7 (perspicuity):** "All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear unto all; yet those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed, for salvation, are so clearly propounded and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of the ordinary means, may attain unto a sufficient understanding of them."
- **1.8:** The Hebrew Old Testament and Greek New Testament are "authentical; so as in all controversies of religion the Church is finally to appeal unto them." They are to be translated "into the vulgar language of every nation."
- **1.9 (analogy of Scripture):** "The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself; and therefore, when there is a question about the true and full sense of any scripture (which is not manifold, but one), it must be searched and known by other places that speak more clearly."
- **1.10:** "The Supreme Judge, by which all controversies of religion are to be determined, and all decrees of councils, opinions of ancient writers, doctrines of men, and private spirits, are to be examined, and in whose sentence we are to rest, can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture."

  - One of his concerns is TIS's tendency to give pre-critical (patristic, medieval, Reformation) exegesis more credibility than contemporary exegesis.

- **Counter-critique.** A critic argues that Carson's selection of TIS writers was too narrow to represent the movement's main proponents. — [Secundum Scripturas, Feb 2012](https://secundumscripturas.com/2012/02/)

### Inferences
- **Three tiers of checks in the confessions.** First, the *analogy of Scripture*: compare passages, letting the clearer explain the less clear. Second, the *analogy or rule of faith*: no reading may contradict "any principal point of our faith." Third, the *rule of charity*: no reading may contradict the law of love. TIS's "rule of faith" is in effect a recovery of the second tier. An AI can use the ecumenical creeds (Apostles', Nicene) plus the Irenaean/Tertullianic rule as a **boundary check** that all four of the user's traditions accept, while leaving distinctives (Calvinist/Arminian, sacraments, church order) open and labelled.
- **Tradition: ministerial, not magisterial.** WCF 1.10 and Second Helvetic 2.2 model how Margin should treat Henry, Spurgeon, Wesley and the Fathers. They are honoured guides, presented respectfully and accurately, but tested by Scripture and allowed to disagree with one another. By the same logic the AI's own output is one more of the "doctrines of men" examined under 1.10.
- **Calibrated confidence.** WCF 1.7 implies three levels:
  - matters necessary for salvation, which are clear and can be stated confidently;
  - many other matters, which are "not alike plain" and should be presented with appropriate tentativeness, laying out the main options;
  - obscure texts, which should be explained from clearer ones (1.9).
- **The AI is not illumination.** WCF 1.6 says the Spirit's inward illumination is necessary for saving understanding. The AI should present itself as an aid among the "ordinary means," not a replacement for prayerful reading, the church or preaching.
- **One sense vs. the Fathers' spiritual senses.** WCF 1.9's "true and full sense… not manifold, but one" stands against the medieval four-fold sense. When the AI voices Church Fathers who allegorize, it should label the reading as allegorical or spiritual and set it beside the literal/historical sense, not merge the two.

- **Catholic parallels not researched.** For the user's Catholic interest this matters: e.g., Dei Verbum §12 and the Catechism's criteria and four senses. Another researcher may cover it.

## 6. Wesleyan approaches: the "quadrilateral," Wesley's "analogy of faith," and the preface to the *Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament*

### Takeaway
"Wesleyan Quadrilateral" (Scripture, tradition, reason, experience) is a modern label coined by Albert Outler. Wesley never used it, and Outler publicly regretted coining it because it was "so widely misconstrued" as making four equal authorities. Scholars stress that for Wesley Scripture was primary, read through the "analogy of faith": the connection and harmony of the core saving doctrines of original sin, justification by faith, the new birth, and inward and outward holiness. His *Notes* deliberately keep commentary short and plain for lay readers, and lean heavily on J. A. Bengel's *Gnomon*.

### Cited Findings
- **Outler's regret.** Outler, "The Wesleyan Quadrilateral—in John Wesley," *Wesleyan Theological Journal* 20:1 (1985), p. 16: "The term 'quadrilateral' does not occur in the Wesley corpus—and more than once I have regretted having coined it for contemporary use since it has been so widely misconstrued." — [Ted Campbell, *WTJ* 41:2 (2006), citing Outler](https://wesley.nnu.edu/fileadmin/imported_site/wesleyjournal/2006-wtj-41-2.pdf); [blog quoting the 1985 article](https://curtsite.wordpress.com/2015/05/12/the-myth-and-the-abuse-of-the-wesleyan-quadrilateral/); [*WTJ* 20:1 (1985) PDF](https://wesley.nnu.edu/fileadmin/imported_site/wesleyjournal/1985-wtj-20-1.pdf)
- **What he regretted.** The term gave the impression that all four elements were equal. Outler was careful to say Wesley held Scripture to be the supreme authority, able to overrule the other three. (This is a secondary report.) — [Kevin M. Watson](https://kevinmwatson.com/2013/05/13/experience-in-the-so-called-wesleyan-quadrilateral/); [Methodist Message (Singapore)](https://www.methodist.org.sg/methodist-message/the-wesleyan-quadrilateral-what-is-it-and-is-it-relevant-today/); [EBSCO Research Starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/wesleyan-quadrilateral/)
- **"Modern Methodist myth."** Ted A. Campbell, "The 'Wesleyan Quadrilateral': The Story of a Modern Methodist Myth," *Methodist History* 29:1 (Jan. 1991), 87–95. — [Headway Ireland article bibliography](https://www.headwayireland.com/cms/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Article-5.-Wesleyan-Quadrilateral.pdf); [blog bibliography](https://curtsite.wordpress.com/2015/05/12/the-myth-and-the-abuse-of-the-wesleyan-quadrilateral/)
- **When the term was coined is disputed.** Outler claimed to have invented it, apparently around 1970. Other sources say 1964 or "the 1960s." One scholar (Peter Doble) reports hearing it from R. Newton Flew in the 1950s. — [Headway Ireland](https://www.headwayireland.com/cms/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Article-5.-Wesleyan-Quadrilateral.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesleyan_Quadrilateral)
- **The debate is live in 2026.** A July 2026 blog post is titled "The Wesleyan Quadrilateral needs to go." — [David Swisener, 5 Jul 2026](https://davidswisener.com/2026/07/05/the-wesleyan-quadrilateral-needs-to-go/)
- **Preface to the NT *Notes* (1755).** Wesley kept the notes "as short as possible, that the comment may not obscure or swallow up the text," and as plain as possible to help the unlearned reader, avoiding critical inquiries and the learned languages. On meeting the work of "Bengelius" he changed his plan: he translated many of Bengel's *Gnomon Novi Testamenti* notes and abridged many more, leaving out the purely critical material. — [Wesley Center Online, Preface to the NT Notes](https://wesley.nnu.edu/john-wesley/john-wesleys-notes-on-the-bible/preface-to-the-new-testament-notes/); [Bible Researcher, "Wesley's New Testament, 1755"](https://www.bible-researcher.com/wesley-nt.html)
- **Intended readers.** Wesley wrote for laypeople studying for their own spiritual walk, while consulting "men of learning," Bengel chief among them. — [*Asbury Journal* 73/1 (2018)](https://place.asburyseminary.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2418&context=asburyjournal)
- **Preface to the OT *Notes*, item 4.** Readers should keep "a constant eye to the analogy of faith," meaning the connection and harmony among the fundamental doctrines of original sin, justification by faith, the new birth, and inward and outward holiness. — [Wesley Center Online, Preface to the OT Notes](https://wesley.nnu.edu/john-wesley/john-wesleys-notes-on-the-bible/preface-to-the-old-testament-notes/); [CCEL, Wesley's Notes](https://ccel.org/ccel/wesley/notes/notes.ii.i.html)
- **Wesley and the Reformers.** Like the Protestant Reformers, Wesley held that faithful reading must accord with the analogy of faith (Rom 12:6): Scripture's clear central content, its teaching on salvation, is the standard by which every interpretation is judged. His note on Romans 12:6 makes the same point. — [G. Sujin Pak, "John Wesley and the Protestant Reformers on Scripture," Oxford Institute of Methodist Theological Studies (2018)](https://oxford-institute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/07_2018_pak.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Scripture first, three aids.** For a Wesleyan-flavoured mode, the AI should treat Scripture as primary authority. Tradition (the Fathers, the Anglican formularies), reason and Christian experience are aids for understanding and confirming it, not co-equal sources. That matches Outler's own account and the Reformed confessions (WCF 1.10).
- **Wesley's "analogy of faith" is soteriological and holiness-centred.** It differs in emphasis from the Westminster "analogy of Scripture," which is textual comparison. An AI voicing Wesley (or Adam Clarke, the main Wesleyan commentator) should foreground grace, new birth, assurance and sanctification. Where Calvinist commentators (Henry, Spurgeon) read the same text differently, especially on election, perseverance or atonement, it should say so rather than smooth the difference over.
- **Wesley's design principle suits audio.** "The comment may not obscure or swallow up the text": short, plain notes subordinate to Scripture.
- **Wesley's *Notes* are derivative.** They lean on Bengel, so many "Wesley" readings of the NT are really mid-18th-century critical judgments. Their textual and philological claims are dated, while their theology remains Wesley's.

- **Not retrieved:** Scott J. Jones, *John Wesley's Conception and Use of Scripture*; William J. Abraham's critiques of the quadrilateral; the 1972 United Methodist *Discipline* adoption history.

## 7. How did Matthew Henry, Charles Spurgeon, Albert Barnes and Adam Clarke describe their methods, and what are the strengths and limits of using them today?

### Takeaway
- **Henry** stated a frankly practical and devotional aim. He would not force a doctrine out of every verse but add remarks "for doctrine, reproof, instruction," promote "practical godliness," avoid "doubtful disputation," and ask of every text "What is this?" and "What is this to us?"
- **Spurgeon's** *Commenting and Commentaries* is the classic 19th-century evangelical guide to these older works. He ranks Henry "first among the mighty for general usefulness," praises Gill's rabbinic learning, values Clarke's criticism but flags his Arminianism, and rates Barnes as useful but lesser, originally written for Sunday-school teachers.

Their strengths are devotional warmth, canonical and Christ-centred reading, and practical application. Their limits are pre-modern lexical, textual and historical scholarship, confessional partisanship, occasional spiritualizing, and (for Henry) volumes completed by others. I found little modern peer-reviewed assessment because the search budget ran out (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Strengths to lean on.**

  - These commentators read the canon as a unity centred on Christ (Henry: the Old "shows us Jesus Christ"; WCF 7.5).
  - They apply Scripture warmly and practically, in line with WLC 157 and 159.
  - Their prose is quotable and sounds good read aloud.
- **Limits to flag in-product, briefly and without condescension:**
  - *Lexical method.* All of them wrote before Barr (1961), so their etymological and "the Greek word means…" arguments need the cautions in §2.
  - *Historical and textual knowledge.* It is dated. Wesley explicitly omitted Bengel's critical material, and Clarke and Barnes, though learned for their day, predate later discoveries.
  - *Confessional partisanship.* Henry, Spurgeon and Gill are Calvinist; Clarke and Wesley are Arminian. Spurgeon himself told readers to use Clarke "with discretion."
  - *Spiritualizing or allegorizing.* This was said of Gill, and is common in the Fathers, so readings should be labelled under WCF 1.9.
  - *Authorship.* Henry's Romans–Revelation volumes are not wholly Henry's own, so in-app attribution should say so.
- **Voicing a commentator.** When the AI voices one of them it should:
  - stay within his documented positions and tone;
  - quote or cite the commentary location rather than invent "in-character" claims;
  - say plainly when a question (for example, a modern ethical issue) is one the commentator never addressed, and mark any extrapolation as extrapolation;
  - offer a short "modern scholarship note" where the commentator's factual claim is now widely disputed.

  This follows from the confessions' "test the Fathers" stance (Second Helvetic 2.2) and Spurgeon's own discriminating use of commentaries.

## 8. Concrete, defensible rules for an AI Bible-study assistant (agreed vs. contested)

### Takeaway
Most of what the standard works teach becomes a short, defensible rule set. Six points have broad consensus across Puritan/Reformed, Wesleyan, evangelical-academic and early-church-respecting Protestant traditions:
- text first;
- original meaning before application;
- context in widening circles;
- genre awareness;
- disciplined word study;
- tradition as a tested guide.

Five points are genuinely contested within the user's traditions and should be presented as such rather than settled by the AI:
- how and how often to make Christ connections from OT texts;
- whether "principlizing" is the right bridge to application;
- the status of allegorical or spiritual senses and any "fuller sense";
- how much authority creeds and tradition carry;
- Calvinist vs. Arminian readings.

### Cited Findings
The anchor statements the rules rest on, all sourced above:
- **Original meaning governs.** "A text cannot mean what it could never have meant for its original readers/hearers." — [Logos (Fee & Stuart)](https://www.logos.com/product/50318)
- **Grammatico-historical method; Scripture interprets Scripture; literary forms matter.** — [Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, Art. XVIII](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/chicago_statement_on_biblical_inerrancy.json); [Chicago Hermeneutics Art. XV](https://www.alliancenet.org/the-chicago-statement-on-biblical-hermeneutics)
- **One sense; clearer texts explain less clear; Scripture is the supreme judge over "opinions of ancient writers."** — [WCF 1.9–1.10](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/westminster_confession_of_faith.json)
- **Interpret from the language, the circumstances, like and unlike passages, and the rule of faith and love; honour the Fathers but dissent where they contradict Scripture.** — [Second Helvetic ch. 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/second_helvetic_confession.json)
- **No interpretation against "any principal point of our faith… or yet unto the rule of charity."** — [Scots Confession ch. 18](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/scots_confession.json)
- **Not all things are "alike plain," but what is necessary for salvation is clear; the Spirit's illumination is needed.** — [WCF 1.6–1.7](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/westminster_confession_of_faith.json)
- **Reading needs attention to "matter and scope… meditation, application, self-denial, and prayer"; hearers "examine what they hear by the scriptures."** — [WLC 157, 160](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NonlinearFruit/Creeds.json/master/creeds/westminster_larger_catechism.json)
- **"Analogy of faith" = harmony of original sin, justification, new birth, holiness; and "that the comment may not obscure or swallow up the text."** — [Wesley, OT Notes preface](https://wesley.nnu.edu/john-wesley/john-wesleys-notes-on-the-bible/preface-to-the-old-testament-notes/); [Wesley, NT Notes preface](https://wesley.nnu.edu/john-wesley/john-wesleys-notes-on-the-bible/preface-to-the-new-testament-notes/)
- **Word-study fallacies:** root fallacy, semantic anachronism, illegitimate totality transfer. — [9Marks on Carson](https://www.9marks.org/review/book-review-exegetical-fallacies-by-d-a-carson/); [Poythress on Barr](https://byfaithweunderstand.com/2008/11/07/poythress-summarizes-james-barr/page/2/?et_blog)
- **Principle tests:** in the text, timeless, not culture-bound, consistent with Scripture, relevant to both audiences. — [Duvall & Hays via Ethnos360](https://e360bible.org/how-to-study-the-bible/the-interpretive-journey/)
- **"Authoritative in expounding biblical principles, but tentative in applying them."** — [Stott via LibraryThing review](https://www.librarything.com/work/1528833)
- **One big idea per sermon, "a bullet, not a buckshot."** — [Robinson via SharperIron](https://sharperiron.org/node/35527)
- **FCF:** the shared human condition that needs "the grace of the passage." — [Chapell via SharperIron review](https://sharperiron.org/node/14383)
- **Seven legitimate routes from the OT to Christ, after the original setting is established.** — [Greidanus via Themelios](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/preaching-christ-from-ecclesiastes-foundations-for-expository-sermons/)
- **"What is this?" and "What is this to us?"; avoid "doubtful disputation."** — [Henry via Christian Study Library](https://www.christianstudylibrary.org/article/matthew-henry-aim-exposition)
- **Outler regretted the "quadrilateral" because it was misread as four equal authorities.** — [Campbell, *WTJ* 2006](https://wesley.nnu.edu/fileadmin/imported_site/wesleyjournal/2006-wtj-41-2.pdf)

### Inferences
**Proposed rules.** Tag key: **[C]** = broad consensus across the cited traditions; **[M]** = majority evangelical position with recognized dissent; **[X]** = contested, so present options.

1. **[C] Text first; the comment never swallows the text.** Every answer or episode names the passage (book, chapter, verse) and grounds claims in its wording before any commentator speaks. Commentary is shorter than, and subordinate to, Scripture. *(Wesley's NT preface; WCF 1.10; Second Helvetic 2.)*
2. **[C] "Then" before "now."** State what the passage meant to its original audience before giving any principle or application, and label the two moves separately ("What it meant / What it means for us"). *(Fee & Stuart; Duvall & Hays steps 1→5; Osborne; Kaiser & Silva; Chicago XV.)*
3. **[C] Context in widening circles.** Work from sentence to paragraph to book to author to Testament to canon. Exegete the passage before bringing in cross-references, and use clearer passages to explain obscure ones, never the reverse. *(WCF 1.9; SharperIron; Aquila Report; Osborne.)*
4. **[C] Name the genre and apply its rules.** Use the table in §3: narrative describes unless the text signals a norm, and God is the hero; law is covenant; psalms are prayer; proverbs are general wisdom; prophecy speaks first to its own hearers; apocalyptic speaks in images; epistles are occasional. Treat hyperbole, round numbers, topical order and free quotation as conventions, not errors. *(Fee & Stuart; ICBI XIII, XV.)*
5. **[C] Disciplined word study.** Define a word by its use in this context. Pick one sense rather than stacking all of them. No etymological "literally means," and no explaining Greek or Hebrew by later English derivatives. Keep word and concept apart. Report a commentator's word study as his, with a caution where modern lexicography differs. *(Barr; Carson; Silva.)*
6. **[C] Calibrate confidence to clarity.** Separate (a) what the text plainly says, (b) the mainstream reading, (c) live disagreements among faithful interpreters, giving the main positions fairly, and (d) the AI's own speculation. Avoid "doubtful disputation" unless the user asks. *(WCF 1.7; Henry; Stott's authoritative/tentative distinction.)*
7. **[C] Tradition guides, Scripture judges.** Present the Fathers, Puritans, Wesleyans and 19th-century scholars accurately and respectfully. Show where they agree and disagree, and dissent "modestly" where a reading contradicts the plain sense. *(Second Helvetic 2.2; WCF 1.10; Belgic 7; Scots 18.)*
8. **[C] Rule of faith and charity as a boundary check.** Never offer, as the meaning of a text, a reading that contradicts the ecumenical creedal core (Trinity, incarnation, atonement, resurrection, return of Christ) or the law of love. Treat confessional distinctives as distinctives. *(Irenaeus; Tertullian; Second Helvetic; Scots 18; Wesley's analogy of faith.)*
9. **[M] Derive application through stated principles.** A principle must be in the text, not culture-bound, consistent with all of Scripture, and relevant to both audiences. Be confident about the principle and tentative about specific modern applications; offer applications as questions for reflection rather than directives. *(Duvall & Hays; Stott. Contested: Barkley's critique that principlizing is presented as the only model and is too individualistic.)*
10. **[M] One big idea per teaching unit.** Each study episode states its main idea in one sentence near the start and repeats it at the end. *(Robinson; WLC 159 "plainly.")*
11. **[X] Christ and redemptive history.** Show how the passage fits the storyline that culminates in Christ, using a *named* legitimate route (Greidanus's seven: redemptive-historical progression, promise-fulfilment, typology, analogy, longitudinal themes, NT references, contrast). Do it only after the original meaning is clear. Label typology as typology, and do not manufacture links. *(Greidanus; WCF 7.5–6; Henry. Contested: how far "Christ in every text" should go; the redemptive-historical vs. exemplary debate.)*
12. **[X] Grace-shaped application.** Connect the text's human need to God's provision (Chapell's FCF) rather than bare moralism, while still naming sin and duty plainly. *(Chapell; WCF 19.6. Contested: WLS critique that the FCF can soften sin.)*
13. **[X] Senses beyond the literal.** When a Church Father or older commentator allegorizes or spiritualizes, present it as "a spiritual/allegorical reading by X." Keep it distinct from the historical sense, and do not treat it as the text's single meaning. *(WCF 1.9 "not manifold, but one"; Fee & Stuart "not allegories"; the Gill spiritualizing critique. Contested: Catholic and patristic four-fold sense; sensus plenior per Kunjummen.)*
14. **[X] Calvinist/Arminian and other confessional splits.** On election, perseverance, the extent of the atonement, sacraments, baptism, eschatology and the Sabbath, present the Reformed reading (Henry, Spurgeon, WCF) and the Wesleyan reading (Wesley, Clarke) side by side. Do not default to one unless the user has chosen a tradition. *(Spurgeon's "use Clarke with discretion"; Outler and Pak on Wesley.)*
15. **[C] Commentator persona fidelity.** A voiced commentator speaks only from his documented work. The AI cites the commentary location, never invents quotations, flags extrapolation beyond the commentator's corpus, and adds a short modern-scholarship note where his factual claim (etymology, history, science, textual basis) is now widely disputed. *(Inference from Second Helvetic 2.2, §2 and §7.)*
16. **[C] The AI is an aid, not an oracle.** Encourage the listener's own reading, prayer, meditation and self-examination, and their local church and preaching. Invite them to "examine what they hear by the scriptures." Never present the AI's output as illumination or authority. *(WCF 1.6–1.7; WLC 155–160.)*
17. **[C] Scripture is primary among sources.** When drawing on tradition, reason or experience, do not treat them as co-equal with Scripture. *(Outler's own regret; WCF 1.10; Belgic 7.)*
18. **[C] Avoid proof-texting.** Never cite a verse for a claim its context does not support. When building a doctrinal point across texts ("good and necessary consequence," WCF 1.6), show the reasoning step by step. *(Proof-texting critiques; Cornerstone RBC on WCF 1.6.)*

**Implementation note** (an inference for prompt design). Rules 1–8 and 15–18 can be hard constraints in the system prompt. Rules 9–14 work better as house style plus "present options" behaviour, with a user setting for preferred tradition (Reformed / Wesleyan / Catholic-patristic / mixed) that changes which reading leads but never hides the alternatives.
