## 1. Catholic teaching: Dei Verbum (1965), PBC *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993), *Verbum Domini* (2010), Catechism §§101–141, Aquinas ST I q.1 a.10, and developments in 2025–26

### Takeaway
Official Catholic teaching asks for two things at once:
- **Study the human text historically.** This means attending to literary forms, the author's intention and critical method. The Pontifical Biblical Commission (PBC) calls the historical-critical method "indispensable".
- **Read it theologically.** This means reading in light of the unity of all Scripture, the living Tradition and the "harmony" of the faith (the analogy of faith). Final judgment belongs to the Church's teaching office, which "is not above the word of God, but serves it."

The literal sense is the foundation, and the allegorical, moral and anagogical senses are built on it. Both fundamentalist literalism and faith-less, "secularized" exegesis are explicitly rejected.

### Cited Findings
**Dei Verbum (Second Vatican Council, Dogmatic Constitution on Divine Revelation, 18 Nov. 1965)**
- **§12, the human side.** To find what the sacred writers intended, the interpreter must attend to "literary forms." Truth is "set forth and expressed differently in texts which are variously historical, prophetic, poetic, or of other forms of discourse." — [Dei Verbum §12, vatican.va](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html); the same wording appears in the [Adoremus text](https://adoremus.org/2007/12/dei-verbum/) and the [Diocese of Fort Worth PDF](https://fwdioc.org/dei-verbum-vatican-II-english.pdf)
- **§12, the divine side (the three criteria).** Scripture must be read in the Spirit in which it was written. So attention is owed to the content and unity of the whole of Scripture, and "The living tradition of the whole Church must be taken into account along with the harmony which exists between elements of the faith." Commentators summarize the three criteria as (1) the content and unity of Scripture, (2) the living Tradition, and (3) the harmony or analogy of faith. — [Adoremus](https://adoremus.org/2007/12/dei-verbum/); [Fort Worth PDF](https://fwdioc.org/dei-verbum-vatican-II-english.pdf)
- **§12, the closing clause.** All interpretation is "subject finally to the judgment of the Church, which carries out the divine commission and ministry of guarding and interpreting the word of God." — [Dei Verbum §12](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html)
- **§10.** "This teaching office is not above the word of God, but serves it, teaching only what has been handed on." The task of "authentically interpreting the word of God, whether written or handed on, has been entrusted exclusively to the living teaching office of the Church." And: "Sacred tradition and Sacred Scripture form one sacred deposit of the word of God, committed to the Church." — [Dei Verbum §10](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html); the last sentence is quoted by [Pope Leo XIV, General Audience, 28 Jan. 2026](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/audiences/2026/documents/20260128-udienza-generale.html)

**Catechism of the Catholic Church, Part One, Article 3 "Sacred Scripture" (§§101–141)**
- **§112:** "Scripture is a unity by reason of the unity of God's plan, of which Christ Jesus is the center and heart, open since his Passover." **§113:** Scripture is read within "the living Tradition of the whole Church." — [CCC (Catholic Culture)](https://catholicculture.org/culture/library/catechism/index.cfm?recnum=460); [CCC §§101–141 (scborromeo.org)](https://www.scborromeo.org/ccc/p1s1c2a3.htm); [USCCB flipbook](https://www.usccb.org/sites/default/files/flipbooks/catechism/34/)
- **§115:** There are two senses, literal and spiritual. The spiritual sense is subdivided into allegorical, moral and anagogical. — [CCC, vatican.va IntraText](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PQ.HTM)
- **§116:** The literal sense is the meaning conveyed by the words, found by exegesis that follows the rules of sound interpretation. "All other senses of Sacred Scripture are based on the literal." — [CCC, vatican.va](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PQ.HTM); [CCC 101–141 PDF](https://stjudesp.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/CCC-101-141.pdf)
- **§117:** The spiritual sense rests on the fact that, because God's plan is one, the realities and events the text speaks of can themselves be signs. Its three forms:
  - allegorical: e.g., the Red Sea crossing as a sign of Baptism
  - moral: leading us to act justly
  - anagogical: e.g., the Church on earth as a sign of the heavenly Jerusalem

  — [CCC (Catholic Culture, recnum 467)](https://www.catholicculture.org/culture/library/catechism/index.cfm?recnum=467)
- **§118** cites the medieval distich that begins "Littera gesta docet…" (the letter teaches events; allegory, what to believe; the moral sense, what to do; anagogy, where we are heading). Latin wordings vary between sources. A tertiary source traces the literal, allegorical and moral modes to Origen and the anagogical to John Cassian (c. 360–435). That is a simplification; treat it with caution. — [CCC, vatican.va](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PQ.HTM); [Wikipedia, "Four senses of Scripture"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_senses_of_Scripture)
- **§119:** Exegetes are to work by these rules toward a better understanding of Scripture, so that their research helps the Church form a firmer judgment. — [CCC, vatican.va](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PQ.HTM)

**Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q.1, a.10 ("Whether in Holy Scripture a word may have several senses")**
- **Body of the article.** The first meaning of the words is the literal sense. The spiritual sense is the meaning by which "things signified by words have themselves also a signification." It is "based on the literal, and presupposes it." — [ST I q.1 a.10, CCEL (English Dominican Province translation)](https://ccel.org/ccel/aquinas/summa.FP_Q1_A10.html); [Latin and English at aquinas.cc](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.I.Q1.A10.C)
- **Reply to Objection 1.** Multiple senses do not make Scripture equivocal. "All the senses are founded on one — the literal — from which alone can any argument be drawn, and not from those intended in allegory, as Augustine says (Epis. 48)." And "nothing necessary to faith is contained under the spiritual sense which is not elsewhere put forward by the Scripture in its literal sense." (Latin: *nihil sub spirituali sensu continetur fidei necessarium, quod Scriptura per litteralem sensum alicubi manifeste non tradat*.) — [CCEL](https://ccel.org/ccel/aquinas/summa.FP_Q1_A10.html); [aquinas.cc](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.I.Q1.A10.C)

**Pontifical Biblical Commission, *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993)**
- **Status and date.** The document was presented to Pope John Paul II on 23 April 1993. — [Evangelization Station pamphlet (PDF)](https://evangelizationstation.com/oldsite/Pamphlets/401%20Fundamentalist%20Interpretation%20of%20Scripture.pdf)
- **On method.** "The historical-critical method is the indispensable method for the scientific study of the meaning of ancient texts. … its proper understanding not only admits the use of this method but actually requires it." — [PBC full text (catholic-resources.org)](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp-FullText.htm); [CCJR copy](https://www.ccjr.us/dialogika-resources/documents-and-statements/roman-catholic/vatican-curia/pbc-1993)
- **Ratzinger's preface.** Cardinal Ratzinger notes that the method carried hidden dangers as well as positive possibilities. — [Catholic Culture copy](https://www.catholicculture.org/culture/library/view.cfm?recnum=7795)
- **Older roots of the method.** The document traces elements of it to Greek commentators on classical literature and, "in the course of the patristic period," to Origen, Jerome and Augustine. — [PBC full text](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp-FullText.htm)
- **"Approaches based on tradition."** The document lists three: the canonical approach, recourse to Jewish traditions of interpretation, and the history of the influence of the text (*Wirkungsgeschichte*). On the last it says: "This appropriation of the text, which can occur either on the individual or community level and can take shape in various spheres (literary, artistic, theological, ascetical and mystical), contributes to a better understanding of the text itself." — [PBC Part I (catholic-resources.org)](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp1.htm)
- **On fundamentalism (I.F).** The document grants that fundamentalism is right to insist on "the divine inspiration of the Bible, the inerrancy of the word of God" and related truths. But it says the way these are presented is "rooted in an ideology which is not biblical." It calls the approach dangerous because it attracts people who want ready answers from the Bible, offering interpretations that are pious but illusory and a false certitude. It also says: "Without saying as much in so many words, fundamentalism actually invites people to a kind of intellectual suicide." — [Goodreads quotation page](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/12199317-the-fundamentalist-approach-is-dangerous-for-it-is-attractive-to); [Evangelization Station pamphlet](https://evangelizationstation.com/oldsite/Pamphlets/401%20Fundamentalist%20Interpretation%20of%20Scripture.pdf); [BAS Library excerpt, "Fundamentalist Interpretation is 'Naively Literalist'"](https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/sidebar/fundamentalist-interpretation-is-naively-literalist-an-excerpt-from-the-catholic-report-on-the-bible/)
- **On ancient and patristic spiritual exegesis.**
  - "Persuaded that the mystery of Christ offers the key to interpretation of all Scripture, ancient exegesis labored to find a spiritual sense in the minutest details of the biblical text." And: "Whatever its pastoral usefulness might have been in the past, modern exegesis cannot ascribe true interpretative value to this kind of procedure."
  - The Fathers "felt themselves at liberty to take a sentence out of its context in order to bring out some revealed truth which they found expressed within it. In apologetic directed against Jewish positions or in theological dispute with other theologians, they did not hesitate to rely on this kind of interpretation."

  — [PBC full text](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp-FullText.htm)
- **On actualization (Part IV).** Applying the text to the present is necessary, but "Actualization, therefore, cannot mean manipulation of the text." — [PBC Part IV (catholic-resources.org)](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp4.htm)
- **Reception and critique.** *Biblical Archaeology Review* summarized the document as endorsing modern critical scholarship and condemning fundamentalist interpretation. Lewis Ayres and Stephen Fowl question the "necessary priority" it gives the historical-critical method; this is a contested point. — [BAS Library](https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/article/the-catholic-church-and-bible-interpretation/); [Ayres & Fowl, "(Mis)reading the Face of God: *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church*," *Theological Studies* 60 (1999)](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/60.3.6.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Catholic teaching is not anti-critical.** In official Catholic terms, explaining historical setting, genre and authorship questions is not "liberal." The PBC calls the method "indispensable," and Leo XIV says Scripture is human language. An AI host should not frame critical context as un-Catholic, and should not present Catholic reading as fundamentalist.
- **Keep "literal" and "literalist" apart.** In Catholic and Thomist usage, the "literal sense" means the meaning the words convey, including genre and metaphor. It is not "literalist" (PBC; VD 44). The app's vocabulary should keep the two terms distinct.
- **Aquinas offers a built-in safety rail.** His rule is that doctrinal argument comes only from the literal sense, and that nothing necessary to faith depends only on a spiritual reading. That lets the AI offer allegorical, typological and moral readings from Henry, Spurgeon or the Fathers as traditional or devotional readings, while tying any doctrinal claim to the plain sense of some passage. Protestant interpretive principles share this instinct (§7).
- **The final-judgment clauses are a Catholic distinctive.** DV 10 and 12 should be reported as Catholic teaching ("Catholic teaching holds…"), not adopted as the app's own rule of authority.
- **A design rule for voicing commentators.** *Antiqua et Nova*'s warnings about anthropomorphism and deception support three rules for the "AI voicing a commentator" feature:
  - clearly and repeatedly disclose that this is an AI reconstruction
  - ground what the persona says in retrieved texts
  - never fabricate quotations

## 2. The Orthodox approach: Scripture within Tradition, the patristic mind, liturgical and church-centered reading

### Takeaway
Orthodox interpretation places Scripture inside the Church's living Tradition: liturgy, the Fathers, councils and icons. Three points follow:
- **Tradition is Scripture rightly understood.** It is not a second, separate source of doctrine.
- **Following the Fathers means acquiring their "mind"** (*phronema*), not piling up quotations.
- **The Old Testament is received in its Septuagint form,** and believers meet much of Scripture in worship.

Leading Orthodox biblical scholars (Breck, Stylianopoulos, Pentiuc, Behr) engage modern critical scholarship but place it beneath patristic *theoria* (spiritual contemplation of the text) and the Church's Christ-centered reading.

### Cited Findings
- **Georges Florovsky, *Bible, Church, Tradition: An Eastern Orthodox View* (Collected Works, vol. 1).** A summary of these essays describes Tradition as Scripture rightly understood rather than a supplementary source of other doctrines, and says the Church possesses the correct interpretation of the Scriptures. — [Florovsky, *Bible, Church, Tradition* (PDF)](https://www.bulgarian-orthodox-church.org/rr/lode/florovsky1.pdf); [Goodreads summary/review](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/4824291-bible-church-tradition)
- **Florovsky on the two Testaments.** It was "a traditional idea of patristic exegesis" that the Word of God was "revealing himself continuously, and in divers manners, throughout the whole of the Old Testament." Yet no strict analogy holds between the Testaments, because their "covenantal situations" are profoundly different. They relate as figure and truth. — [Florovsky PDF](https://www.bulgarian-orthodox-church.org/rr/lode/florovsky1.pdf)
- **Florovsky's method.** For him, following the Fathers means acquiring their mind (*phronema*), not simply quoting them. A commentator describes this as a theological method well grounded in Scripture, the ancient Fathers and the liturgy. — [*International Journal of Orthodox Theology* 7:1 (2016)](https://d-nb.info/1191205304/34); [Orthodox Christian Laity, "Georges Florovsky and the Mind of the Fathers"](https://ocl.org/georges-florovsky-and-the-mind-of-the-fathers/)

- **Canon.** The Orthodox OT follows the Septuagint and includes books called *anagignoskomena* ("things that are read" or "worthy of reading"). Totals vary by jurisdiction; some lists add 3–4 Maccabees, Psalm 151 or the Prayer of Manasseh. — [BibleProject, "Why the Deuterocanon / Apocrypha Is in Some Bibles and Not Others"](https://bibleproject.com/articles/why-deuterocanon-apocrypha-some-bibles-and-not-others/); [FaithTime (popular source, low authority)](https://www.faithtime.ai/content/bible-lessons/how-many-books-are-in-the-orthodox/)

### Inferences
- **Present each Father as one witness.** For Orthodox users, authority rests in the Church's living Tradition, experienced above all in worship, not in any single Father. An AI voicing Chrysostom or Augustine should present him as one witness within that Tradition. It should claim "the Fathers teach…" only when the texts show broad agreement. This follows Florovsky's "mind of the Fathers," as opposed to collecting proof-texts.
- **Flag Septuagint differences and canon status.** Orthodoxy reads a Septuagint-form OT and a wider canon. The app should flag places where the Greek OT differs from Hebrew-based English translations; the Greek is what most Fathers used, Chrysostom's citations included. It should also mark how each tradition classifies a given deuterocanonical or *anagignoskomena* book.
- **Same structure as the Catholic model, different authority.** Orthodox scholars engage historical criticism but place it under patristic *theoria* and liturgical reading. This mirrors the Catholic "two levels" model in structure, but Orthodoxy has no single teaching office equivalent to DV 10.
- **Margin's patristic sources are Western channels.** The Catena Aurea is a Latin medieval compilation; the NPNF volumes are Victorian Anglican and American translations. They only partly convey how Orthodoxy experiences the Fathers, which is through liturgy and the Greek originals. The app should not present them as "the Orthodox view."

### Gaps
- **No primary text read in full.** No primary text by Florovsky, Breck, Stylianopoulos or Behr was read in full, so specific quotations are unverified. Examples:
  - Florovsky's "Tradition was in the Early Church, first of all, a hermeneutical principle and method" ("The Function of Tradition in the Ancient Church," 1963).
  - "The Church is equally committed to the Kerygma of the Apostles and to the dogmata of the Fathers." This appeared in a search excerpt, but I could not pin down the source essay.
- **Not checked:**
  - official Orthodox confessional texts, such as the Confession of Dositheus / Synod of Jerusalem (1672) and the 1848 Encyclical of the Eastern Patriarchs
  - Vincent of Lérins's classic test of consensus (*quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus*: what has been believed everywhere, always, by all)
- **Critiques not retrieved.** Critiques of Florovsky's neo-patristic program (e.g., Paul Gavrilyuk, 2013) and of "patristic consensus" as a construct were not retrieved. One excerpt mentioned an "easternocentric" (anti-Western) bias critique, but its source was unclear.
- **Not verified:** details of the liturgical lectionary, Holy Cross articles by Stylianopoulos, and the *Orthodox Study Bible*.
- **Weak sourcing on canon counts.** Orthodox canon totals come only from popular sources.

## 3. Patristic exegesis, and how modern patristics scholars recommend reading the Fathers today

### Takeaway
The Fathers shared a few premises about Scripture:
- it is a single, Christ-centered, prophetic and morally formative whole
- it is read under the Church's "rule of faith" (Irenaeus)
- it is read for the sake of love of God and neighbor (Augustine)

Origen's body-soul-spirit scheme and the Antiochene concern for the coherence of the narrative are differences of emphasis within that shared world, not two rival "methods." Modern scholars (Young, Wilken, Hall, Oden) recommend reading the Fathers in their context, as conversation partners, without copying their allegory wholesale and without anachronistic proof-texting.

### Cited Findings
**Irenaeus (late 2nd century)**
- ***Against Heresies* 1.8.1.** The Gnostics are like someone who takes a jeweled mosaic of a king, rearranges the stones into a dog or fox, and insists the animal is the real portrait. Their system is one the prophets did not announce, the Lord did not teach, and the apostles did not hand down. — [AH 1.8, New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103108.htm); [transcription at churchofgodtwincities.org](http://churchofgodtwincities.org/links/irenaeus.htm)
- ***Against Heresies* 1.9.4.** Whoever keeps unchanged the "rule of truth" received at baptism will recognize the scriptural names and phrases the heretics use, but not their arrangement. Scripture must be read in its proper order and connection. Putting each passage back in its place within the "body of truth" exposes the false reading. — [AH Book 1, Roberts–Donaldson translation (Early Christian Writings)](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/irenaeus-book1.html); [Christian Leaders reading guide](https://christianleaders.org/mod/page/view.php?id=31090)
- **The rule of faith and the creeds.** The rule (one God, one Son, one Holy Spirit) is described as the basis of the later creeds. This is a secondary characterization. — [Christian Leaders](https://christianleaders.org/mod/page/view.php?id=31090)

**Origen, *De Principiis* (On First Principles) 4.2.4**
- "As man is said to consist of body, and soul, and spirit, so also does sacred Scripture." The simple are built up by the "body" (the plain or historical sense), those who have progressed by the "soul," and the perfect by the "spiritual law." Some passages have no "bodily" sense at all. The English renders Rufinus's Latin, which may differ from Origen's Greek. — [De Principiis IV, New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04124.htm); [Early Christian Writings](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/origen125.html)
- **Modern summaries.** The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* explains that Origen matched the reader's body, soul and spirit to levels in the text. A review of *The Soul and Spirit of Scripture within Origen's Exegesis* describes the somatic/psychic/pneumatic (body/soul/spirit) scheme as dominant in biblical interpretation until the Reformation; that is a broad and contestable generalization. — [SEP, "Origen"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/origen/); [review on ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236778697_The_Soul_and_Spirit_of_Scripture_within_Origen's_Exegesis_review)

**Augustine, *De Doctrina Christiana* (On Christian Teaching)**
- **1.36.40:** "Whoever, then, thinks that he understands the Holy Scriptures, or any part of them, but puts such an interpretation upon them as does not tend to build up this twofold love of God and our neighbor, does not yet understand them as he ought." — [On Christian Doctrine (NPNF translation), Catholic Library](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?docId=%2FSynchronized-EN%2FAugustine.OnChristianDoctrine.en.html&chunk.id=00000077); [Bluffton University text](https://www.bluffton.edu/courses/humanities/1/xndoct.htm); [CCEL PDF](https://ccel.org/ccel/a/augustine/doctrine/cache/doctrine.pdf)
- **Same context (1.36):** someone who reaches a reading that builds up love, while missing the author's exact meaning, errs, but "his error is not pernicious, and he is wholly clear from the charge of deception." — same sources
- **Book 2 (ch. 6):** obscure passages do not threaten Scripture, since "hardly anything may be found in these obscure places which is not found plainly said elsewhere." — same sources
- **Secondary characterization.** Augustine "delimits polysemy by the rule of faith, the twofold love commandment, and clearer canonical texts." A meaning the human author did not intend "is not necessarily wrong." — [Sam Davidson, "The Imprint of De Doctrina Christiana in the First Chapter of the Summa Theologiae" (Substack)](https://samdavidson.substack.com/p/the-imprint-of-de-doctrina-christiana)

**Alexandria versus Antioch: current scholarship**
- **Frances M. Young, *Biblical Exegesis and the Formation of Christian Culture* (Cambridge University Press, 1997; Baker Academic reissue, 2002).** It replaces the habit of sorting patristic exegesis into discrete methods (moral, typological, allegorical) and schools (Alexandrian, Antiochene) with a more nuanced account. — [Baker Academic](http://bakerpublishinggroup.com/books/biblical-exegesis-and-the-formation-of-christian-culture/332590); [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/2923121-biblical-exegesis-and-the-formation-of-christian-culture)
- **John J. O'Keefe's review in the *Journal of Early Christian Studies*.**
  - Young refuses to reduce patristic exegesis to "Alexandria vs Antioch" or "allegory vs typology."
  - The prominence of these distinctions "reveals more about modern intellectual concerns than it does about the ancient exegetical situation."
  - There is no "allegory" versus "typology," only several styles, and the terms were already problematic in antiquity.

  — [O'Keefe review (Project MUSE)](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/10045)
- **Review in *Shofar*.** Shared premises (Scripture as prophetic, and as moral *paideia*, i.e. formation) led to different ways of "imitating" the biblical narrative:
  - Alexandrians practiced a "symbolic mimesis": Scripture's words carry the reader to a higher spiritual narrative.
  - Antiochenes practiced an "iconic mimesis": they guarded the coherence of the biblical narrative while drawing models of virtue from it.

  The ancients "simply did not know the modern craving for a purely native historical meaning." — [*Shofar* review (Project MUSE)](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/472850/summary)
- **Contested.** Not every reviewer accepts Young's rehabilitation of allegory. One reviewer in the search results (probably a Goodreads reader review) holds that many habitual allegorists "promoted far-fetched interpretations," while not writing off the Alexandrians as bad readers. Popular explainers still use the Antioch/Alexandria contrast. — [Goodreads reviews](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/2923121-biblical-exegesis-and-the-formation-of-christian-culture); [Center for Baptist Renewal, "What Was the Antiochene School of Interpretation?" (2022)](https://www.centerforbaptistrenewal.com/blog/2022/12/2/what-was-the-antiochene-school-of-interpretation)

**Modern patristics scholars on reading the Fathers today**
- **Robert Louis Wilken, "In Defense of Allegory," *Modern Theology* 14, no. 2 (1998): 197–212, doi:10.1111/1468-0025.00063.** Reprinted as "In Defence of Allegory" in *Theology and Scriptural Imagination* (Blackwell, 1998). The debate continues, e.g. Peter W. Martens, "Reconsidering the critique of allegory," *Modern Theology* 28, no. 4 (2012). Wilken also wrote *The Spirit of Early Christian Thought* (Yale, 2003). — [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/WILIDO-11); [Wiley table of contents](https://gov.wiley.com/WileyCDA/WileyTitle/productCd-063121075X,descCd-tableOfContents.html); [Wikipedia, "Robert Louis Wilken"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Louis_Wilken)
- **Christopher A. Hall, *Reading Scripture with the Church Fathers* (Downers Grove: InterVarsity Press, 1998; 225 pp.).**
  - It introduces evangelical readers to the Fathers' world and how they read Scripture.
  - Jeffrey Greenman's endorsement says Hall "helps us to read the church fathers contextually and not anachronistically."
  - Reviewers sum up its thesis as making the Fathers routine conversation partners in interpretation. One reviewer notes Hall is critical of allegorical exegesis and of Origen.

  — [IVP](https://www.ivpress.com/reading-scripture-with-the-church-fathers); [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Reading_Scripture_with_the_Church_Father.html?id=AluN24fEDywC); [Goodreads reviews](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/550111.Reading_Scripture_with_the_Church_Fathers); [Puritanboard review](https://puritanboard.com/threads/reading-scripture-with-the-church-fathers-hall.85062/)
- **Thomas C. Oden (gen. ed.), *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture* (ACCS; IVP, 29 vols., from 1998).**
  - **How passages were chosen:** patristic writings were searched with computer help, then excerpts were hand-picked for "salient insight, rhetorical power, and faithful representation of the consensual exegesis of the early church."
  - **Scope:** from Clement of Rome to John of Damascus (mid-8th century), including parts of the deuterocanonical books.
  - **Aim:** Oden's standard is the Christian consensus of the first millennium. The series means "not to replace" 20th-century commentaries and is described as a "postcritical" revival of the medieval *glossa ordinaria* tradition.
  - **Editors:** Wikipedia describes them as confessionally collaborative (Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic, Protestant and Jewish scholars).

  — [IVP ACCS set](https://www.ivpress.com/accs-paperback-set); [Wikipedia, "Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Christian_Commentary_on_Scripture); [Ransom Fellowship review (1998)](https://ransomfellowship.org/article/ancient-christian-commentary-on-scripture-editor-thomas-c-oden-1998/)
- **Contested point.** Allegory's value is itself disputed. The PBC denies "true interpretative value" to finding spiritual meanings in minute details, Wilken mounts a "defense of allegory," and Hall is cautious about Origen. — [PBC full text](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp-FullText.htm); [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/WILIDO-11); [Goodreads reviews of Hall](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/550111.Reading_Scripture_with_the_Church_Fathers)

### Inferences
- **Drop the textbook binary.** The AI host should not repeat "Alexandrian allegory vs Antiochene literalism"; Young and her reviewers show it is a modern construct. It should describe what a particular Father is doing (a Christ-centered reading, moral formation, attention to the coherence of the narrative, typology) and why.
- **Two strong baseline candidates.** Irenaeus's "rule of truth" and Augustine's test of "twofold love" come before the denominational splits; Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants all claim them. Together they say: read the parts by the whole, read the obscure by the plain, and read toward love of God and neighbor.
- **Patristic limits on spiritual reading.** Augustine limits spiritual readings by love and the rule of faith; Aquinas ties doctrine to the literal sense. The AI can offer allegorical readings while labeling them as such and naming the plain-sense teaching they rest on.
- **Curated collections are not consensus.** Collections built to show "consensus" (ACCS, and the Catena) are curated by design. The AI should not treat what they include as a statistical "patristic consensus." It should say when a reading is idiosyncratic, as some of Origen's are.
- **Avoid anachronism.** Reading "contextually, not anachronistically" (Hall) means the AI should not make the Fathers answer modern questions as if they had addressed them, such as inerrancy debates, evolution, or Reformation disputes over justification.

### Gaps
- **Not verified (search budget exhausted):**
  - Diodore of Tarsus's distinction between *theoria* and *allegoria*.
  - Theodore of Mopsuestia's attack on allegorists (on Gal 4:24), and his condemnation after his death at Constantinople II (553).
  - Chrysostom's characteristic features as an exegete: moral application, *synkatabasis* (divine "condescension"), *akribeia* (precision).
- **Augustine passages not re-checked:**
  - *De Doctrina* 1.2.2: things versus signs.
  - *De Doctrina* 3.10.14: the rule that whatever cannot be referred to purity of life or soundness of doctrine is to be read figuratively.
- **Irenaeus passages not retrieved:**
  - AH 1.10.1: the text of the rule of faith.
  - AH 3.3–3.4: apostolic succession as the guarantee of true teaching.
- **Arguments not retrieved:**
  - The substance of Wilken's "In Defense of Allegory." My recollection is that it grounds allegory in Paul and in the Bible's Christ-centered unity; this is unverified.
  - Hall's own stated cautions.
  - The wording of Oden's ACCS General Introduction.

## 4. The Catena Aurea and the 19th-century patristic translations in Margin's corpus: how they were made, what they are like, and their limits

### Takeaway
The Catena Aurea is a 13th-century Latin "chain" commentary on the four Gospels, compiled by Thomas Aquinas at the commission of Pope Urban IV. It strings together abridged, sometimes paraphrased excerpts from Latin and Greek authorities, including:
- medieval Byzantine commentators
- the *Glossa Ordinaria*, a standard medieval annotated Bible
- the pseudonymous *Opus Imperfectum* ("Pseudo-Chrysostom")

The 1841–45 Oxford translation, edited by J. H. Newman, renders an 18th-century Latin edition into Victorian English. Greek Fathers therefore reach the English reader through at least two translations, and both attributions and wording need caveats. The NPNF volumes of Chrysostom and Augustine are likewise 19th-century translations, some from the Tractarian Oxford Movement, and their style is archaic in places on purpose.

### Cited Findings
**How the Catena was compiled**
- **Commission and sources.** Pope Urban IV commissioned it, wanting an orthodox patristic commentary available to scholars. Sources include Ambrose, Origen, Augustine, Cyril of Alexandria, Gregory the Great and Bede. — [Wikipedia, "Catena (biblical commentary)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catena_%28biblical_commentary%29)
- **Dedications.** Newman's preface describes the work as dedicated to Urban IV. The dedication of the Mark portion is addressed to Cardinal Annibaldo Annibaldi, cardinal-priest of the Basilica of the Twelve Apostles (a Dominican cardinal from 1262, died 1272; one source says 1276). Standard accounts give Matthew to Urban IV and Mark, Luke and John to Annibaldo, but I could not verify this split independently here. — [Newman Reader, Catena Aurea preface](https://www.newmanreader.org/works/fathers/catena.html); [Catena on Mark, dedication (Jon Haines transcription)](https://jonhaines.com/thomas/CAMark.htm); [Wikipedia, "Annibaldi family"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annibaldi_family)
- **Dates.** It is usually dated 1262–1268. The Mark, Luke and John portions belong to the period when Aquinas directed the Dominican house of studies at Santa Sabina in Rome (c. 1265). — [TextManuscripts (Les Enluminures)](https://www.textmanuscripts.com/medieval/thomas-aquinas-catena-aurea-326753); [Wikipedia, "Catena"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catena_%28biblical_commentary%29); [Wikipedia, "Pontifical University of Saint Thomas Aquinas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifical_University_of_Saint_Thomas_Aquinas)
- **Greek sources.** In the Mark dedication, Aquinas says he had certain Greek commentaries translated into Latin and inserted among the Latin doctors, with the authors' names noted. Scholarship identifies Victor of Antioch, Nicetas of Heraclea and Theophylact among the Greek works used. So "Fathers" here stretches to medieval Byzantine commentators. — [Jon Haines (Mark dedication)](https://jonhaines.com/thomas/CAMark.htm); ["The Doctor Communis of the Sacred Page: Aquinas's Catena aurea and the preservation of Greek patristic and Byzantine exegetical tradition" (academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/109360782/The_Doctor_Communis_of_the_Sacred_Page_Aquinass_Catena_aurea_and_the_preservation_of_Greek_patristic_and_Byzantine_exegetical_tradition)
- **Did Aquinas read Greek?** Newman's preface argues he probably did. Treat this as Newman's opinion, not settled fact. — [Newman Reader](https://www.newmanreader.org/works/fathers/catena.html)

**Reliability problems the 19th-century translators themselves flagged**
- **"Chrysostom" is often not Chrysostom.** The *Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum*, cited as "Chrysostom" (that is, Pseudo-Chrysostom), is "largely employed" in the Catena. In Matthew it is cited fully until about the middle of chapter 8 and more rarely after that. Erasmus refuted its attribution to Chrysostom in 1530; its author was probably an Arian (candidates include a Constantinopolitan priest named Timothy). — [Henry Wace, *Dictionary of Christian Biography*, "Pseudo-Chrysostomus" (CCEL)](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/wace/biodict.html?term=Pseudo-Chrysostomus); [Catena Aurea on Matthew, Oxford translation (isidore.co)](https://isidore.co/aquinas/english/CAMatthew.htm); [Wikipedia, "Opus Imperfectum"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_Imperfectum)
- **The translators' own warnings.**
  - "Many of the passages ascribed to S. Chrysostom are not found in the works of that Father."
  - The Catena's text was corrupted in copying, so "the names of their authors are often in doubt."
  - Aquinas often kept the sense rather than the words, and "has in several instances quite missed the sense of the Greek."

  — [Oxford translators' preface (ecatholic2000)](https://www.ecatholic2000.com/catena/untitled-04.shtml); [isidore.co](https://isidore.co/aquinas/english/CAMatthew.htm)
- **Other sources mixed in.** The *Glossa Ordinaria* is drawn on throughout Matthew, alongside Jerome, Hilary and most of Chrysostom's genuine Matthew homilies. — [isidore.co](https://isidore.co/aquinas/english/CAMatthew.htm); [ecatholic2000 preface](https://www.ecatholic2000.com/catena/untitled-04.shtml)

**The 1841 Oxford translation**
- **Publication.** Edited by John Henry Newman and translated from the 1775 Venetian edition of the Latin text. Published by John Henry Parker (Oxford) in four volumes, 1841–1845; Matthew came first (1841). The Matthew volume has Newman's roughly five-page preface on the genre and significance of the work. The translation is sometimes wrongly credited to Parker. — [Logos, Catena Aurea vol. 1](https://www.logos.com/product/9710/catena-aurea-commentary-on-the-four-gospels-collected-out-of-the-works-of-the-fathers-volume-1-st-matthew); [Medieval Sources Bibliography](https://medievalsourcesbibliography.org/sources.php?id=2146116562)
- **Later editions.** Baronius Press announced a reprint in 2009; one source reports a 2013 edition, still in print, and the listing names Aidan Nichols as a contributor. A single bibliography reports that a new Aquinas Institute translation is planned for 2028; this is unconfirmed. — [The Hermeneutic of Continuity (2009)](https://the-hermeneutic-of-continuity.blogspot.com/2009/12/catena-aurea-from-baronius-press.html); [Amazon listing](https://www.amazon.com/Catena-Aurea-Commentary-Collected-4/dp/1901157407); [Medieval Sources Bibliography](https://medievalsourcesbibliography.org/sources.php?id=2146116565)

**The NPNF (Schaff) and Oxford "Library of the Fathers" translations of Chrysostom and Augustine**
- **The Oxford "Library of the Fathers."** Pusey planned the *Library of Fathers of the Holy Catholic Church* in 1836, and Pusey, Keble and Newman signed its prospectus. Some 45–50 annotated volumes came out from J. H. Parker between 1838 and the 1880s; sources disagree on the exact count and end date. It belongs to the Oxford Movement's effort to ground Anglican doctrine in the authority of the Fathers. Newman's *via media* held that the Anglican rule of faith was fidelity to the Fathers. — [Wikipedia, "Library of the Fathers"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_the_Fathers); [list of titles (tertullian.org)](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/lfc_list.htm); [Oxford Reference](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803100104145); [Encyclopedia.com, "Oxford Movement"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/philosophy-and-religion/christianity/protestant-denominations/oxford-movement)
- **Schaff's NPNF.** The *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, First Series (14 vols., Buffalo: Christian Literature Co., 1886–1889/90), covers Augustine and Chrysostom. For example, NPNF1-03 is Augustine's *On the Holy Trinity* and NPNF1-10 is Chrysostom's *Homilies on Matthew*. — [Wikipedia, "Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicene_and_Post-Nicene_Fathers); [CCEL NPNF1-03](https://ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf103.html); [CCEL NPNF1-10 (PDF)](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/s/schaff/npnf110/cache/npnf110.pdf)
- **Uneven style across volumes.**
  - The Chrysostom *Matthew* volume says: "The archaic style of the English translation has been preserved without material alteration. Even when obscure and involved, the form seemed to be a fitting dress for the original." Spelling was modernized.
  - In the *Acts/Romans* volume, Schaff "improved" the Oxford version: "archaic and obsolescent words or expressions have often been replaced by more idiomatic modern language," and inaccurate Bible quotations were replaced with Revised Version or critically based renderings.

  — [CCEL NPNF1-10 (PDF)](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/s/schaff/npnf110/cache/npnf110.pdf); [Liberty Fund OLL, NPNF set](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/schaff-a-select-library-of-the-nicene-and-post-nicene-fathers-of-the-christian-church-14-vols)

### Inferences
- **Treat the Catena as reception, not source.** It is a medieval Western *reception* of the Fathers, not their own words. Each excerpt has passed through, in order:
  1. Aquinas's selection and abridgment
  2. a 13th-century Latin translation (for Greek authors)
  3. possible copying errors
  4. an 18th-century Latin edition
  5. 1840s English
- **Attribution guardrail.** When the AI cites "Chrysostom" from the Catena on Matthew, especially chapters 1–8, it should check whether the line comes from the genuine *Homilies on Matthew* (in NPNF1-10) or from the *Opus Imperfectum*. If the latter, label it "Pseudo-Chrysostom (*Opus Imperfectum*, probably by an Arian author)." The same care applies to:
  - "Gloss" (the *Glossa Ordinaria*, a medieval compilation, not a Father)
  - Byzantine authors such as Theophylact, who appear alongside the Fathers
- **Cross-check against NPNF.** Where Margin also holds the NPNF text of Chrysostom or Augustine, the AI should quote the fuller NPNF passage and use the Catena mainly as an index pointing to it.
- **Mark the language layer.** Archaic Victorian English, sometimes kept on purpose, tempts paraphrase. The app must mark paraphrase versus quotation. An AI "voicing" Chrysostom in modern English should say it is paraphrasing a 19th-century translation of the Greek.
- **The channels have their own history.** These texts reached Margin through an Anglican Tractarian project, an American Protestant project and a Catholic medieval compilation, each with its own interests (e.g., Newman's *via media*). That is itself a reception-history fact worth showing users.

### Gaps
- **Not verified:**
  - the names of the individual 1841–45 translators (as I recall, including Mark Pattison, J. D. Dalgairns and T. D. Ryder)
  - the exact number of authors the Catena cites (often given as about 80)
  - the full wording of Newman's preface beyond the excerpts
- **Adversus Judaeos in NPNF?** Unverified. I believe NPNF does not include Chrysostom's *Adversus Judaeos*; the full English is Harkins's *Discourses Against Judaizing Christians* in the Fathers of the Church series.
- **Augustine translations.** I did not establish which NPNF Augustine volumes come from the Oxford Library or how heavily they were revised.
- **Catena reliability scholarship.** No modern critical studies of the Catena's sources were retrieved beyond the 19th-century prefaces and one academia.edu article.
- **2028 translation.** The planned Aquinas Institute translation rests on a single source.

## 5. Reception history (Wirkungsgeschichte) and responsible use of historic commentaries, including anachronism, proof-texting, and anti-Jewish or polemical material

### Takeaway
Reception history is now an established academic field. Examples include the Wiley Blackwell Bible Commentaries, De Gruyter's *Encyclopedia of the Bible and Its Reception*, and Ulrich Luz's work on Matthew; the Catholic PBC also endorses "Wirkungsgeschichte" as a legitimate approach. The field treats historic commentators as witnesses to how texts were read and lived in their own settings, with good and harmful effects alike, not as timeless authorities.

The main pitfalls are:
- anachronism
- proof-texting out of context, which the Fathers themselves practiced
- curated "consensus"
- polemical anti-Jewish material

Mainstream church documents (Nostra Aetate 4; PBC 2001) and scholars (Wilken on Chrysostom; Luz on Matt 27:25) handle that material through historical context and explicit repudiation, not by suppressing it.

### Cited Findings
**The field**
- **Wiley Blackwell Bible Commentaries.** Described as "the first to be devoted primarily to the reception history of the Bible," the series "is based on the premise that how people have interpreted, and been influenced by, a sacred text like the Bible is often as interesting and historically important as what it originally meant." It emphasizes the Bible's influence on literature, art, music and film, on religious belief and practice, and on social and political developments. ### Inferences
- **Present each commentator as a witness.** Reception history gives Margin's core idea a recognized scholarly frame: reading Scripture alongside Henry, Spurgeon, Chrysostom, Augustine and Aquinas's Catena. Each voice should appear as a witness with a date and place: who, when, where, for whom, and against whom. The Blackwell premise supports attaching a short historical note to each commentator.
- **"History of effects" includes harms.** Some texts have a documented harmful history, e.g. Matt 27:25, John 8:44, 1 Thess 2:14–16, and "the Jews" in John. For these, the AI should be ready to say so, citing mainstream church statements (Nostra Aetate 4; PBC 2001) and scholarship (Luz). It should neither repeat the polemic in its own voice nor silently censor the historic commentator.
- **Hard rule for the Chrysostom persona.** An AI voicing Chrysostom must not be prompted into producing new anti-Jewish invective "in character." The persona may explain, in a framing note, what the historical figure said and why it is now repudiated; it does not act it out.
- **No patristic proof-texts for modern positions.** The PBC itself notes the Fathers' habit of using verses out of context. Quotations should come with the surrounding argument and the genre (homily, polemic, commentary).
- **Apply the guardrails symmetrically.** Reformation-era and later Protestant commentaries can contain supersessionist (the view that the Church has replaced Israel), anti-Catholic or anti-Jewish polemic too; see Gaps. Applying the rules evenly across traditions is part of being fair to all of them.

## 6. Mainstream academic method (historical-critical, literary, canonical), its relation to confessional reading, and examples of convergence across traditions

### Takeaway
Historical-critical scholarship works as common ground among the churches. The Catholic PBC calls it "indispensable." Catholic, Lutheran, Anglican, Reformed and Methodist exegetes have produced joint studies that moved questions which once divided churches:
- *Peter in the New Testament* (1973)
- *Mary in the New Testament* (1978)
- *The Biblical Foundations of the Doctrine of Justification* (2012)

Yet every confessional tradition, and the "theological interpretation" movement, insists that this method is necessary but not sufficient. The Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (JDDJ) models how to state agreement honestly as "differentiated consensus": agreement in basic truths, with the remaining differences named.

### Cited Findings
- **The PBC on method.** The historical-critical method is "indispensable." Ratzinger's preface warns of hidden dangers. The document also recognizes "approaches based on tradition" (canonical; Jewish traditions of interpretation; *Wirkungsgeschichte*) and rejects fundamentalism. — [PBC full text](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp-FullText.htm); [Catholic Culture](https://www.catholicculture.org/culture/library/view.cfm?recnum=7795)
- **A critique of that priority.** Ayres and Fowl (1999) contest the "necessary priority" the PBC gives historical criticism; their critique comes from the theological-interpretation side. — [*Theological Studies* 60 (1999)](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/60.3.6.pdf)
- **Verbum Domini §35.** It warns that exegesis without faith becomes "positivistic and secularized," and calls for a synthesis of historical reason and faith. — [Verbum Domini](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/apost_exhortations/documents/hf_ben-xvi_exh_20100930_verbum-domini.html)
- **Luz on reception history.** Reception history is presented as moving "beyond the limitations of the historical-critical method." — [Goodreads, *Matthew in History*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/3494830-matthew-in-history)
- **The Scripture Project (Center of Theological Inquiry, Princeton): Ellen F. Davis and Richard B. Hays (eds.), *The Art of Reading Scripture* (Eerdmans, 2003).**
  - After four years of meetings, scholars and pastors set out "Nine Theses on the Interpretation of Scripture" (pp. 1–5). The aim was to move beyond a purely historical-critical approach toward theological reading.
  - Thesis 1 presents Scripture as truthfully telling the story of God's creating, judging and saving action.
  - Thesis 8 calls Christians to "read the Bible with diverse others outside the church," including Jews and critics.
  - One reviewer notes that some contributors tacitly disagree with the theses.

  — [Eerdmans](https://www.eerdmans.com/9780802812698/the-art-of-reading-scripture/); [Antony Billington's blog (lists the theses)](http://antony-billington.blogspot.com/2008/12/nine-theses-on-interpretation-of.html); [DTS review](https://voice.dts.edu/review/ellen-f-davis-the-art-of-reading-scripture/)

### Inferences
- **Lead with the shared academic layer.** A widely accepted, roughly "neutral" academic layer exists: historical setting, genre, textual and translation issues, and connections between texts. The AI host can begin there as common ground before introducing tradition-specific readings. That mirrors how the ecumenical dialogues worked (the Peter and Mary volumes; the 2012 justification study).
- **Neutrality has limits.** Catholic (VD 35), Orthodox (Florovsky, Breck) and Protestant theological-interpretation voices (Davis and Hays; Ayres and Fowl) all reject treating historical criticism as the whole of interpretation. In a devotional app, critical findings should appear as one layer ("scholars note…") alongside the confessional and devotional readings users come for. Critical skepticism should be neither the default authority nor suppressed.
- **Use JDDJ as a model for contested topics.** "Differentiated consensus" gives the AI a pattern: first state what the traditions affirm together (with sources), then state the remaining distinct emphases, each attributed, without declaring a winner. Methodist and Reformed bodies have associated themselves with the JDDJ, which bears directly on the user's Wesleyan and Reformed interests.
- **Canonical reading fits the app, with a caveat.** The canonical approach reads texts in their final form within the canon. The PBC accepts it as an approach "based on tradition," and it suits an app that reads whole books with commentators. But it presupposes a particular canon (see §7).

## 7. Where traditions genuinely diverge, and which principles can serve as a shared baseline for an AI study assistant

### Takeaway
The deepest divergences are:
- **Who may interpret with authority.** For Catholics, the Magisterium (teaching office). For the Orthodox, the Tradition of councils, Fathers and liturgy. For Protestants, "Scripture interprets Scripture" under the Spirit, with Reformed and Wesleyan forms of that principle.
- **How large the canon is.** 73 books (Catholic), a broader Septuagint-based canon (Orthodox), or 66 books (Protestant).
- **How much weight the spiritual senses carry.**
- **Doctrinal conclusions** on Mary, the Petrine office, the sacraments and justification.

There is still a substantial shared baseline:
- read for the plain or literal sense, in its genre and history
- read the parts by the whole, and the obscure by the clear
- keep the Christ-centered unity of Scripture in view
- aim at love of God and neighbor
- read humbly, with the "communion of saints" (believers past and present)
- listen to others, including Jewish interpreters

### Cited Findings
**Who has authority to interpret**
- **Catholic.** Authentic interpretation is "entrusted exclusively to the living teaching office of the Church," which "is not above the word of God, but serves it." — [Dei Verbum §10](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html)
- **Orthodox.** Tradition is Scripture rightly understood; the Church possesses the right interpretation; following the Fathers means sharing their mind, not trading proof-texts. — [Florovsky PDF](https://www.bulgarian-orthodox-church.org/rr/lode/florovsky1.pdf); [*International Journal of Orthodox Theology* (2016)](https://d-nb.info/1191205304/34)
- **Reformed (Westminster Confession).**
  - 1.9: "The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself." When the true and full sense of a text is in question, it must be searched out from other places that speak more clearly.
  - 1.10: the supreme judge is the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture.

  — [Heidelblog, WCF text](https://heidelblog.net/2014/03/westminster-confession/); [Christian Study Library, "WCF Chapter 1: The Meaning and Interpretation of Scripture"](https://www.christianstudylibrary.org/article/westminster-confession-faith-chapter-1-meaning-and-interpretation-scripture); [RPC Canada, Westminster Standards (PDF)](https://rpccanada.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Westminister-Standards-LetterSized-for-Web.pdf)
- **Wesleyan.** In his *Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament* on Rom 12:6, Wesley reads "analogy of faith" as the "grand scheme of doctrine" running through Scripture: original sin, justification by faith, and present, inward salvation. He stresses "a close and intimate connection between the chief heads of that faith," and every doubtful scripture is to be interpreted in line with them. — [Wesley, *Notes* on Romans 12 (CCEL)](https://ccel.org/ccel/Wesley/notes/notes.i.vii.xiii.html); [sacred-texts.com](https://sacred-texts.com/bib/cmt/wesley/rom012.htm)

**The canon**
- **Book counts.** Catholic Bibles have 73 books (46 OT and 27 NT); Protestant Bibles have 66 (39 and 27). The NT is the same in all traditions.
- **Catholic list.** Trent (1546) affirmed the Catholic list, deuterocanonical books included. It did not accept 3–4 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151 or 1–2 Esdras.
- **Orthodox lists.** Orthodox OT totals are larger and vary (Septuagint-based *anagignoskomena*).
- **Protestant position.** The Westminster Confession says the Apocrypha, "not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the Canon of Scripture."
- **Printing practice (generalization).** Lutheran and Anglican Bibles have often printed the Apocrypha in a separate section; Reformed and Methodist Bibles usually leave it out.

— [BibleProject](https://bibleproject.com/articles/why-deuterocanon-apocrypha-some-bibles-and-not-others/); [St. Austin Catholic Parish, "The Deuterocanonical Books"](https://staustin.org/the-deuterocanonical-books)

**Senses of Scripture**
- **Catholic:** a fourfold sense (CCC 115–118), with every sense based on the literal (CCC 116; Aquinas).
- **Patristic precedent:** Origen's threefold body/soul/spirit scheme.
- **The PBC's limit:** finding spiritual senses "in the minutest details" has no true interpretive value for modern exegesis.

— [CCC, vatican.va](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PQ.HTM); [Aquinas, ST I q.1 a.10](https://ccel.org/ccel/aquinas/summa.FP_Q1_A10.html); [Origen, *De Principiis* IV](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04124.htm); [PBC full text](https://catholic-resources.org/ChurchDocs/PBC_Interp-FullText.htm)

**Principles shared across traditions (independent sources that agree)**
- **Coherence of the whole; the obscure read by the clear:**
  - DV 12 (unity of the whole of Scripture; "harmony… between elements of the faith")
  - CCC 112 (the unity of God's plan, with Christ at its center)
  - WCF 1.9 (unclear places read by clearer ones)
  - Augustine (hardly anything obscure that is not "plainly said elsewhere")
  - Wesley's analogy of faith
  - Irenaeus (restoring each passage to its place in "the body of truth")

### Inferences
**A default baseline the AI can apply regardless of tradition:**
1. **Plain sense first.** Start with the words, genre, historical and literary context, and any translation or textual notes. Label scholarly hypotheses as hypotheses.
2. **Read within the whole.** Place the passage in its book and in the whole canon, explain obscure passages by clearer ones, and point out canonical cross-references.
3. **Note the shared Christ-centered reading.** All three Christian traditions read Scripture as centered on Christ (CCC 112; Irenaeus; Behr; Wesley). Present typology and allegory as historic readings, attributed to their source, not as the plain sense.
4. **Test applications by love of God and neighbor** (Augustine). Refuse readings that license contempt for anyone, including Jews (NA 4; PBC 2001).
5. **Treat commentators as witnesses.** Label each with tradition, era and context. Be explicit when a voice is idiosyncratic and when a claim reflects broad agreement.
6. **Keep four things distinct:** what the text says, what a commentator says, what a tradition teaches, and what the AI itself infers.

**Divergences the AI must respect: describe and attribute them, and never settle them by default.**
- **Who interprets with authority.** The app should not adopt any one tradition's rule as its own neutral rule: not "the Church's judgment" (Catholic), not "the patristic consensus" (Orthodox), not "Scripture alone, interpreted by Scripture" (Reformed/Protestant). Attribute each one: "In Catholic teaching…", "Orthodox tradition…", "The Westminster Confession…", "Wesley…". Let users optionally pick a "home tradition" lens without hiding the others.
- **Canon.** When the user reads, or the AI cites, Tobit, Sirach, Wisdom, Maccabees and similar books, state the book's status in each tradition:
  - Catholic: deuterocanonical and inspired
  - Orthodox: *anagignoskomena*, on a broader list
  - Protestant: Apocrypha, outside the canon but sometimes read

  Differences between the Septuagint and the Hebrew text matter for Orthodox users and for how the Fathers quote Scripture.
- **Senses of Scripture.** Catholic and Orthodox users may welcome allegorical, anagogical and liturgical readings as integral. Many Reformed users will want them flagged and placed under the plain sense. Labeling which sense is in play (literal, typological, allegorical, moral, anagogical) serves both.
- **Doctrinal topics.** Shared exegesis does not settle doctrine on Mary, Peter and the papacy, the sacraments, justification and sanctification, or theosis (the Orthodox teaching of union with God). As the Peter and Mary volumes and the JDDJ show, the honest move is to report agreement and difference side by side.
- **Persona design.** An AI voicing Henry, Spurgeon, Chrysostom or Augustine should:
  - speak within that figure's own tradition and era
  - not be made to endorse another tradition's distinctives, or to rule on modern controversies as if the figure had addressed them
  - disclose that it is an AI reconstruction, consistent with *Antiqua et Nova*
  - ground its claims in retrieved text
  - not "own" text whose attribution is doubtful, such as *Opus Imperfectum* passages under Chrysostom's name in the Catena

### Gaps
- **Confessional texts not verified this session:**
  - Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles: the Apocrypha read "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not "to establish any doctrine."
  - The Methodist Articles of Religion on the canon.
  - Official Orthodox decisions on the canon (Synod of Jerusalem, 1672).
  - The Wesleyan Quadrilateral (Albert Outler's formulation), probably covered in another researcher's notes.
- **Not researched:**
  - detailed sacramental and Marian divergences
  - Orthodox teaching on theosis as a frame for interpretation
  - Catholic and Orthodox views on whether the Septuagint is inspired
